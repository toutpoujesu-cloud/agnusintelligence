Japan AI Governance: Hiroshima Process, Society 5.0 and the Regulatory Framework

Japan has played an outsized role in international AI governance through its G7 presidency in 2023, which produced the Hiroshima AI Process — a voluntary framework for advanced AI systems adopted by G7 governments and subsequently referenced by the EU AI Act. Domestically, Japan pursues a Society 5.0 vision that positions AI as essential infrastructure, with a correspondingly light regulatory touch.

Japan's approach: agile governance and Society 5.0

Japan's AI governance philosophy is explicitly captured in the concept of "agile governance" — a feedback loop between policy, technology development, and social acceptance, designed to allow governance to adapt as rapidly as the technology it governs. This stands in contrast to the EU's ex-ante (before the fact) regulatory approach and reflects Japan's determination not to constrain AI development through premature regulation.

Society 5.0 — Japan's vision for a human-centred society powered by advanced technology — positions AI as essential to addressing Japan's demographic challenges (ageing population, labour shortage) and industrial competitiveness. This creates strong domestic political pressure for AI adoption and against restrictive governance.

Japan does not have an AI Act. The Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI) has published AI governance guidelines — most recently the AI Governance Guidelines Version 1.1 (2022) — that establish principles and a governance framework for AI development and deployment. These are voluntary. Japan's legal framework for AI is primarily addressed through existing laws: the Act on the Protection of Personal Information (APPI) for data governance, and sector-specific regulations for high-risk applications.

The Hiroshima AI Process

Japan's G7 presidency in 2023 produced the Hiroshima AI Process — a set of voluntary principles and a code of conduct for advanced AI developers. Adopted by G7 leaders in October 2023 and subsequently endorsed by additional countries and multilateral organisations, the Hiroshima Process represents the most significant multilateral agreement on AI governance to date.

The Hiroshima Process International Guiding Principles for Advanced AI cover: transparency and accountability; AI safety and trustworthiness; respect for human rights and democratic values; privacy protection; information sharing; responsible use; and AI governance. The Code of Conduct for Advanced AI is a voluntary commitment for AI developers to: conduct risk identification and mitigation; invest in cybersecurity; report incidents; develop technical mechanisms for identifying AI-generated content; and support international efforts to develop technical standards.

The Hiroshima Process is referenced in the EU AI Act's recitals as part of the international context for the Act. It also influenced the UK AI Safety Summit outcome documents. For AI governance practitioners, the Hiroshima Process represents the closest thing to a global consensus document on advanced AI governance as of September 2026.

Domestic regulatory framework for AI

APPI and AI: The Act on the Protection of Personal Information (amended 2020, effective 2022) governs AI processing of personal data. The Personal Information Protection Commission (PPC) has published AI-specific guidance covering anonymisation, pseudonymisation, and the conditions under which personal data may be used for AI training. Japan's APPI framework is less restrictive than EU GDPR — it does not include a right not to be subject to automated decisions equivalent to GDPR Article 22, and its legitimate interests equivalent is broader.

METI AI guidelines: The Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry's AI governance guidelines provide a risk-based framework that maps closely to the OECD AI Principles and the NIST AI RMF. They are voluntary but widely referenced by Japanese enterprises and by foreign companies operating in Japan.

Sector regulators: The Financial Services Agency (FSA) has issued guidance on AI in financial services covering model risk management and explainability. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare has guidance on AI in medical devices. The Ministry of Education has guidance on AI in educational settings.

EU adequacy decision

Japan has an EU adequacy decision for personal data transfers, adopted January 2019. This was the first adequacy decision outside EEA countries with close institutional ties to EU data protection. The decision enables organisations to transfer personal data between the EU and Japan without additional safeguards.

The Japan-EU mutual recognition framework for data protection creates some alignment in data governance practices between the two regimes. However, the substantive differences between APPI and GDPR remain significant — particularly around automated decision-making rights and legitimate interests — meaning that a single privacy governance framework does not satisfy both regimes simultaneously.
