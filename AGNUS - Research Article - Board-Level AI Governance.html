AI Governance

Board-Level AI Governance: Oversight, Accountability and Reporting

AI governance that lives only at the technical or operational level is incomplete. Boards bear ultimate accountability for the material risks their organisations create — including risks from AI systems. This article sets out what board-level AI governance requires in practice: what the board needs to know, what it needs to decide, and what it needs to receive as ongoing management information.

Contents

    Why boards are accountable for AI

    Boards are accountable for AI not because they deploy it — in most organisations, the board never touches an AI system directly — but because they have fiduciary and regulatory accountability for the material risks their organisations create. When an AI system causes harm, the accountability chain does not end at the technical team that built it or the business unit that deployed it. It runs up to the board, which had ultimate responsibility for ensuring that material risks were identified, managed, and reported.

    This accountability is being formalised by regulators. The EU AI Act places obligations on providers and deployers at the organisational level — not just the product level. The UK FCA's AI governance expectations treat AI risk as a subset of operational risk, for which boards have existing accountability. The US SEC's cybersecurity disclosure rules (which many legal commentators expect to extend to AI incidents) require board-level disclosure of material cybersecurity risks — and AI incidents increasingly qualify.

    The practical consequence: a board that has delegated all AI governance decisions to a technical committee, with no AI risk visibility at board level, is not meeting its governance obligations — regardless of how good the technical governance below it may be.

    Three questions every board should be able to answer

    Question 1
    What AI systems are we operating — and what are the top three risks?
    If the board cannot answer this without requesting a special briefing, the governance function is not working. The board should receive a standing AI risk summary that covers the live AI system inventory, risk classification, and the current top risks — at least quarterly.
    Question 2
    Have we had any AI incidents in the past quarter — and what did we do about them?
    The board should receive a standing incident report covering any AI-related incidents, near-misses, or regulatory contacts. Not a list of every model run, but a summary of events that crossed defined materiality thresholds. The board should be able to confirm that the response was proportionate.
    Question 3
    Are we compliant with the AI regulations that apply to us — and what is changing?
    The board should understand the regulatory obligations that apply to its AI systems, the current compliance status against those obligations, and what is changing in the regulatory environment. This requires a live regulatory horizon function feeding into the governance report.

    Committee structure and delegation

    Most boards govern AI risk through a committee structure — usually the Audit and Risk Committee, the Technology Committee, or a dedicated AI governance committee. The right structure depends on the organisation's size, the materiality of its AI operations, and the existing committee architecture.

    The critical principle is that delegation does not eliminate accountability. If the board delegates AI governance oversight to the Audit and Risk Committee, the full board remains accountable for the outcomes. The committee must therefore have a clear mandate, a defined reporting line to the full board, and the expertise to exercise genuine oversight rather than rubber-stamp technical recommendations.

    For organisations deploying high-risk AI under the EU AI Act — or AI systems in regulated sectors like financial services or healthcare — an AI-specific committee with appropriate technical expertise is increasingly expected by regulators. The UK FCA, in its AI governance expectations published in 2025, noted that relying on existing risk committees without AI-specific expertise was a governance gap.

    Board-level AI governance roles

    Chief AI Officer or equivalent: For organisations with material AI operations, a named executive-level accountable person — who reports to the board, not just to a committee — is increasingly standard. The CAIO role is not necessarily a separate hire; in many organisations it is a responsibility assigned to the CTO or Chief Risk Officer with explicit AI governance scope.

    Non-executive AI expertise: Boards are expected to have access to AI expertise at the non-executive level. This does not require every non-executive director to be a machine learning engineer — it requires that at least one non-executive has sufficient understanding of AI systems to challenge executive recommendations and assess the adequacy of the governance function.

    What the board should receive

    Effective board-level AI governance requires a structured reporting pack, delivered at least quarterly, covering:

    AI system inventory summary: The number of AI systems in operation, their risk classification (high/medium/low under the applicable framework), and any changes since the last report.

    Risk register highlights: The top AI risks, their current status, and the controls in place. Not every risk — the risks that have changed, crossed a threshold, or require board awareness.

    Incident report: Any AI-related incidents, near-misses, regulatory contacts, or audit findings since the last report. Including the corrective action taken and whether it has been implemented.

    Regulatory update: Changes to the regulatory environment relevant to the organisation's AI systems. This includes new laws, guidance, enforcement actions against comparable organisations, and upcoming implementation deadlines.

    Governance metrics: Key performance indicators for the AI governance function — review cycle times, exception rates, training completion, audit findings closed.

    AI governance and ESG disclosure

    AI governance is increasingly appearing in ESG reporting — particularly for organisations where AI is a material part of the business model or operational risk profile. The EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) and the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) framework both require disclosure of governance arrangements for material risks. For organisations where AI is material, this means AI governance arrangements should be disclosed — at least at the level of who is accountable, what the governance process is, and what the key risks are.

    This creates a discipline effect: if board-level AI governance will be disclosed, boards are more likely to ensure it is adequate. The disclosure requirement is arriving faster than most boards are prepared for.
