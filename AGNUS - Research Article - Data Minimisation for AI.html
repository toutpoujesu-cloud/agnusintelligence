AI Data Governance

Data Minimisation for AI Systems: Principles, Practice and Regulatory Requirements

Data minimisation is a foundational data-protection principle that applies with particular force to AI systems — which have an inherent tendency to collect and process more data than they need. This article explains what data minimisation requires for AI in both training and operational contexts, how to implement it technically, and the consequences of non-compliance under GDPR, the EU AI Act, and sector regulation.

Contents

    The data minimisation principle

    GDPR Article 5(1)(c)
    "Personal data shall be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed."

    Data minimisation is one of the six data protection principles in GDPR Article 5. It requires that personal data collected and processed be limited to what is strictly necessary for the defined purpose. It is not a principle that permits collecting data because it might be useful in the future — data collected without a specific, identified purpose for which it is necessary violates the minimisation principle, regardless of the legal basis under which it was collected.

    For AI systems, data minimisation is more challenging than for traditional software because the relationship between input data and model capability is not always clear in advance. Practitioners often argue that "more data is better" — and in raw performance terms, more data often is better. But performance improvement is not a legal basis for processing personal data, and data collected in excess of what is necessary for the stated purpose is collected unlawfully under GDPR.

    Data minimisation in training

    Data minimisation applies to training data in two ways: the volume of personal data included in the training dataset, and the granularity of that data.

    Volume minimisation

    Training datasets should include personal data only where personal data is necessary to achieve the model's intended purpose. A model trained to detect fraudulent transactions does not need to include data subjects' health information, political opinions, or family relationships — even if such data is technically available in the training corpus. Filtering out unnecessary personal data categories from training datasets before model training is a data minimisation requirement, not an optional quality improvement.

    Granularity minimisation

    Even where personal data is necessary, the granularity of that data should be minimised. If a coarser representation of the data — aggregated, rounded, or partially anonymised — achieves the same model performance, the more granular representation is unnecessary and should not be used. Techniques including k-anonymity, differential privacy, and federated learning can reduce the personal data required to achieve a given level of model capability.

    Data minimisation in operation

    Data minimisation applies not only to training but to the operational use of AI systems. When an AI system processes user inputs in deployment, it should not retain personal data beyond what is necessary for the immediate processing task — and if retention is necessary, it should be for the minimum period and subject to appropriate controls.

    For AI agents and conversational systems, this creates specific challenges. Session context — which the system needs to maintain coherent multi-turn conversation — may contain personal data. How that context is stored, for how long, and who can access it are data minimisation questions that require explicit governance decisions, not default technical behaviour.

    The EU AI Act's data governance requirements

    Article 10 of the EU AI Act supplements GDPR's data minimisation requirement with specific provisions for high-risk AI systems. It requires that training datasets be "relevant" and "sufficiently representative" — but also that personal data processed for AI training be subject to appropriate data governance practices. The combination of GDPR Article 5(1)(c) and AI Act Article 10 creates an interlocking data minimisation obligation: not only must personal data in training be limited to what is necessary, but it must be governed according to defined data management practices that ensure that limitation is maintained throughout the data lifecycle.

    Data Protection Impact Assessment for AI

    A Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is mandatory under GDPR Article 35 where processing is likely to result in a high risk to natural persons — which includes most high-risk AI systems. The DPIA process requires a systematic assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing, including a data minimisation analysis: could the same purpose be achieved with less data, less granular data, or anonymised data?

    DPIAs for AI systems are more complex than standard DPIAs because: (1) the relationship between training data and model capability is probabilistic rather than deterministic; (2) AI systems may process personal data in ways that are not immediately apparent from their specification; and (3) the outputs of AI systems may themselves constitute personal data (model outputs that identify individuals). A well-conducted DPIA for an AI system should address all three of these dimensions.
