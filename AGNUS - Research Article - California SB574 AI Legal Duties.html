California SB 574 imposes confidentiality, accuracy, and disclosure duties on attorneys and arbitrators using generative AI

Enrolled on 4 September 2026, SB 574 would, if signed by the Governor, require California attorneys to verify AI-generated content before use, protect client confidentiality, and prohibit arbitrators from delegating decisions to AI. Awaiting Governor's signature.

Source: California Legislature — official bill status (SRC-0100). SB 574 is enrolled and awaiting the Governor's signature; it is not currently in force. Effective dates and implementing guidance have not been published. This record will be updated when the Governor acts. AGNUS does not provide legal advice.
Contents

    What happened

    California Senate Bill 574 passed both chambers of the California Legislature on 31 August 2026 and was formally enrolled on 4 September 2026. Enrolled status means the bill has been presented to the Governor and is awaiting signature or veto. As of 8 September 2026, the Governor had not yet signed the bill.

    SB 574 would apply to attorneys licensed to practice in California and to arbitrators conducting proceedings subject to California arbitration law. It specifically targets the use of generative AI — systems that generate text, legal arguments, citations, or analysis.

    Legislative status: SB 574 is enrolled — it has passed both chambers and been presented to the Governor. It is not yet signed into law. California's Governor has 30 days after the Legislature adjourns to sign or veto enrolled bills. The Legislature typically adjourns in mid-September, making the signature deadline mid-October 2026. This article will be updated when the Governor acts.

    What SB 574 requires

    If signed, SB 574 creates four principal obligations:

    Duty 1
    Confidentiality protection
    Attorneys must ensure that client confidential information is protected when using generative AI. This means they cannot submit confidential client information to AI systems that use it for model training or that may expose it to third parties, without appropriate safeguards or client consent.
    Duty 2
    Accuracy verification
    Attorneys must verify the accuracy of AI-generated content — including citations, case summaries, legal arguments, and factual statements — before submitting it to a court, arbitration panel, or opposing party. Unverified AI-generated content submitted to legal proceedings would be a breach of this duty.
    Duty 3
    Correction duty
    If an attorney discovers that AI-generated content they have submitted contains errors, they are required to correct the record promptly. This duty applies regardless of whether the error was discovered before or after submission.
    Duty 4
    Court disclosure
    Attorneys must disclose to the court when they have relied on AI-generated information that is outside the existing record. This disclosure obligation is designed to give courts visibility into when AI has been used to generate substantive legal content.

    Arbitration-specific prohibition

    SB 574 includes a separate provision for arbitrators: they are prohibited from delegating decision-making to generative AI. An arbitrator may use AI as a research or drafting aid, but the arbitral decision itself must reflect the arbitrator's own judgment. Disclosure is required before an arbitrator relies on AI-generated information that is not already in the evidentiary record.

    Why this matters

    California is the largest state economy in the United States and a major centre for legal practice. SB 574 would establish California as the first US state with a specific statutory regime for attorney and arbitrator use of generative AI — supplementing existing professional-responsibility rules under the California Rules of Professional Conduct.

    The practical implications for law firms, legal technology vendors, and in-house legal teams with California-facing work are significant. Firms using AI legal research, document drafting, or argument generation tools would need to build verification and disclosure workflows into their practice. General counsel relying on AI tools in California-governed arbitrations would face explicit duties around delegation and disclosure.

    The bill also signals that US state legislatures — and likely federal courts through rule-making — are moving toward express AI-specific professional duties, rather than relying solely on existing professional-responsibility interpretation.

    What organisations should watch

    • Governor's signature or veto — mid-October 2026 deadline
    • If signed, the effective date and any phase-in provisions
    • Whether other US states introduce similar legislation following California's lead
    • California State Bar guidance on how SB 574 interacts with existing professional-responsibility rules
    • Federal court rule-making on AI disclosure requirements (several circuits are already consulting)
