EU AI Act Key Definitions Explained

Article 3 contains 68 statutory definitions. This article covers the thirteen most consequential for compliance: AI system, provider, deployer, operator, intended purpose, reasonably foreseeable misuse, safety component, substantial modification, serious incident, profiling, general-purpose AI model, systemic risk, and downstream provider.

Statutory definitions carry legal weight in both directions — they expand scope and they constrain it. Getting the foundational definitions wrong is the earliest compliance error an organisation can make.
Contents

    Article 3’s definitions underpin every other article in the Regulation. This article does not reproduce all 68 — it covers the thirteen definitions most frequently cross-referenced across the Chapter III obligation set and most commonly misread in practice. The full statutory text is available via the Source Library (SRC-0058).

    Executive summary

    Foundational definitions

    3(1)AI system
    A machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments.

    This is the threshold definition for the entire Regulation. A system that does not meet it is not an AI system and is not subject to any AI Act obligation. The Commission published guidelines on applying this definition in August 2025. Key elements: machine-based; designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy; may be adaptive after deployment; infers outputs from inputs; outputs can influence environments. Rule-based systems that simply execute predetermined logic without inference do not qualify.

    3(3)Provider
    A natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body that develops an AI system or a general-purpose AI model or that has an AI system or a general-purpose AI model developed and places it on the market or puts the AI system into service under its own name or trademark, whether for payment or free of charge.

    The provider is the actor who bears the heaviest obligation set under Chapter III. Note that having a system developed by a third party and then placing it on the market under your own name or trademark makes you the provider — the development need not be internal. Article 25 (AR-000310) can shift provider status mid-lifecycle.

    3(4)Deployer
    A natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body using an AI system under its authority except where the AI system is used in the course of a personal non-professional activity.

    The deployer is the actor using the system — not the one who built it. The personal non-professional carve-out mirrors Article 2(10). A legal entity using an AI system for business purposes is a deployer regardless of size. One entity can be both provider (for systems it places on the market) and deployer (for systems it uses under another provider’s name) simultaneously.

    3(8)Operator
    A provider, product manufacturer, deployer, authorised representative, importer or distributor.

    The catch-all term for all actors in the supply chain. Used in provisions that apply to the full set rather than a specific role. Market surveillance authorities can address measures to any operator.

    Purpose and use

    3(12)Intended purpose
    The use for which an AI system is intended by the provider, including the specific context and conditions of use, as specified in the information supplied by the provider in the instructions for use, promotional or sales materials and statements, as well as in the technical documentation.

    Intended purpose is provider-specified, not deployer-determined. The classification analysis in Article 6 (AR-000163) runs against the intended purpose as the provider has documented it — a deployer who uses a system for a purpose the provider never specified is potentially creating a reclassification situation under Article 25(1)(c). This definition also governs the Article 3(23) substantial-modification test.

    3(13)Reasonably foreseeable misuse
    The use of an AI system in a way that is not in accordance with its intended purpose, but which may result from reasonably foreseeable human behaviour or interaction with other systems, including other AI systems.

    Article 9 (risk management system) requires providers to consider reasonably foreseeable misuse when identifying risks. This definition bounds that obligation: it does not require anticipating all possible misuse, only misuse that is reasonably foreseeable given the system’s design and context.

    3(14)Safety component
    A component of a product or of an AI system which fulfils a safety function for that product or AI system, or the failure or malfunctioning of which endangers the health and safety of persons or property.

    This definition anchors the Article 6(1) product-safety classification route (AR-000163). Whether an AI system is a “safety component” of an Annex I product determines whether the first high-risk classification route applies. The test is functional: does it fulfil a safety function, or would its failure endanger health, safety, or property?

    Lifecycle and modification

    3(23)Substantial modification
    A change to an AI system after its placing on the market or putting into service which is not foreseen or planned in the initial conformity assessment carried out by the provider and as a result of which the compliance of the AI system with the requirements set out in Chapter III, Section 2 is affected or results in a modification to the intended purpose for which the AI system has been assessed.

    Two conditions must both be satisfied: (1) the change was unforeseen or unplanned in the initial conformity assessment, AND (2) compliance is affected OR the assessed intended purpose changes. A pre-planned update that was documented in the initial assessment and does not affect compliance is not a substantial modification, even if technically significant. AR-000310 (Value Chain Responsibilities) applies this definition to the Article 25(1)(b) reclassification trigger.

    3(25)Post-market monitoring system
    All activities carried out by providers of AI systems to collect and review experience gained from the use of AI systems they place on the market or put into service for the purpose of identifying any need to immediately apply any necessary corrective or preventive actions.

    The definition frames the post-market monitoring obligation in Article 72 as an active ongoing programme, not a passive complaint-receipt system. Providers must collect and review experience — both words matter.

    Incidents and risks

    3(2)Risk
    The combination of the probability of an occurrence of harm and the severity of that harm.

    The classical risk definition: probability × severity. Used throughout the Article 9 risk management system and the Article 6(3) derogation test (which requires assessing whether the system “poses a significant risk of harm”).

    3(49)Serious incident
    An incident or malfunctioning of an AI system that directly or indirectly leads to any of the following: the death of a person, or serious harm to a person’s health; a serious and irreversible disruption of the management or operation of critical infrastructure; the infringement of obligations under Union law intended to protect fundamental rights; serious harm to property or the environment.

    The serious incident definition triggers Article 73 reporting obligations. The four categories are conjunctive with “any of” — meeting any one is sufficient. “Directly or indirectly” extends the definition beyond immediate causation. An AI system malfunction that leads indirectly to a fundamental rights infringement qualifies even if the system did not directly infringe those rights.

    Data and biometrics

    3(52)Profiling
    Profiling as defined in Article 4(4) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 [GDPR] — any form of automated processing of personal data consisting of the use of personal data to evaluate certain personal aspects relating to a natural person, in particular to analyse or predict aspects concerning that natural person’s performance at work, economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behaviour, location or movements.

    The AI Act incorporates the GDPR profiling definition by cross-reference. This is directly consequential: Article 6(3)’s derogation from high-risk classification is categorically unavailable to any AI system that profiles natural persons. Getting this definition wrong — particularly assuming profiling requires a formal automated decision rather than any automated evaluation — is one of the most common Article 6(3) mistakes.

    3(41)Remote biometric identification system
    An AI system for the purpose of identifying natural persons, without their active involvement, typically at a distance through the comparison of a person’s biometric data with the biometric data contained in a reference database.

    This definition gates the most heavily regulated Annex III category (point 1(a)) and the enhanced Article 12(3) logging requirements (AR-000151). The critical element is “without their active involvement” — which distinguishes RBI from biometric verification (Article 3(36)), where the person actively presents their biometric data for comparison.

    General-purpose AI

    3(63)General-purpose AI model
    An AI model, including where such an AI model is trained with a large amount of data using self-supervision at scale, that displays significant generality and is capable of competently performing a wide range of distinct tasks regardless of the way the model is placed on the market and that can be integrated into a variety of downstream systems or applications, except AI models that are used for research, development or prototyping activities before they are placed on the market.

    The definition is capability-based (“significant generality,” “wide range of distinct tasks”) rather than architecture-based. The research/development/prototyping carve-out matches the Article 2(6) scope exclusion. AR-000270 (GPAI Obligations) covers the Chapter V obligation set that attaches to providers of these models.

    3(65)Systemic risk
    A risk that is specific to the high-impact capabilities of general-purpose AI models, having a significant impact on the Union market due to their reach, or due to actual or reasonably foreseeable negative effects on public health, safety, public security, fundamental rights, or the society as a whole, that can be propagated at scale across the value chain.

    Systemic risk classification triggers the additional obligations in Article 55 (AR-000270). The definition’s emphasis on “propagated at scale across the value chain” distinguishes systemic risk from the more localised harms that Chapter III’s high-risk provisions address.

    3(68)Downstream provider
    A provider of an AI system, including a general-purpose AI system, which integrates an AI model, regardless of whether the AI model is provided by themselves and vertically integrated or provided by another entity based on contractual relations.

    The downstream provider definition captures the scenario where a GPAI model is integrated into a higher-level AI system. The Article 25(4) written-agreement obligation (AR-000310) applies to this relationship, requiring the provider of the downstream system and the GPAI model provider to establish contractual information-sharing arrangements.

    Open questions

    • Whether the Digital Omnibus has changed any of the 68 definitions — Article 3 carries the Omnibus disclaimer; one or more definitions may have changed. The AI system definition (3(1)) is the most likely candidate given the Omnibus’s scope.
    • Whether the Commission’s August 2025 guidelines on the AI system definition narrow or broaden the Article 3(1) test in practice — the guidelines are linked from the Service Desk but were not reviewed on this research pass.
    • How “significant generality” in the GPAI model definition (3(63)) is assessed quantitatively — Article 51 provides a threshold (10²³ FLOPs for systemic risk), but the “significant generality” threshold for basic GPAI classification is not numerically defined.

    Frequently asked

    FAQ
    Does a rule-based decision system that applies fixed business logic count as an AI system under Article 3(1)?
    Generally no. Article 3(1) requires inference — the system must infer outputs from inputs rather than execute predetermined rules. A system that applies a fixed decision tree without learning or inference from data does not meet the definition. The Commission’s August 2025 guidelines address this boundary in more detail.
    FAQ
    Can the same legal entity be both a provider and a deployer?
    Yes. An organisation that develops and places its own AI system on the market is a provider for that system. The same organisation using a different AI system developed by a third party is a deployer for that system. The roles are system-specific, not organisation-specific.
    FAQ
    Is a planned software update to a high-risk AI system a substantial modification under Article 3(23)?
    Not if it was foreseen and planned in the initial conformity assessment. Article 3(23) requires the change to be “not foreseen or planned” in the initial assessment. A pre-planned update documented in the original assessment falls outside the definition even if technically significant.
