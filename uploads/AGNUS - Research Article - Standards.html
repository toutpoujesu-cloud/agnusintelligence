Harmonised Standards Under the EU AI Act

Article 40 creates a presumption-of-conformity mechanism: a high-risk AI system that complies with a harmonised European standard whose reference has been published in the Official Journal is presumed compliant with the AI Act requirements that standard covers. No AI Act harmonised standards have yet been published in the OJ. Until they are, providers must use other means to demonstrate compliance with Articles 9–15.

The harmonised standards mechanism is the EU’s established conformity shortcut for product regulation. The AI Act inherits it — but the standards themselves do not yet exist in published, OJ-referenced form.

    Builds on AR-000200 (AI Act Explained) and AR-000188 (Technical Documentation). Article 40 defines when harmonised standards satisfy the documentation and risk-management requirements covered in those articles.     Executive summary

    How the mechanism works

    Step 1
    Commission standardisation request
    The Commission issues a standardisation request to CEN, CENELEC, or ETSI specifying which AI Act requirements need coverage. This formally tasks the standardisation bodies with developing the relevant standards.
    Step 2
    Standard developed and published
    The standardisation body (primarily CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 for the AI Act) develops the standard through a technical committee process. Once published as a European Norm (EN), it is available for use but does not yet carry the presumption.
    Step 3
    OJ reference published
    The Commission publishes the standard’s reference in the Official Journal. Only from this point does compliance with the standard create a presumption of conformity with the AI Act requirements it covers.

    Requirements and obligations

    What organizations must do

    • Monitor the Official Journal for publication of AI Act harmonised standard references — when a reference is published, assess whether the standard covers requirements relevant to your system.
    • Track CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 working documents and published Technical Reports as interpretive reference material for Articles 9–15 compliance work, even before OJ-referenced status is achieved.
    • Build your current compliance programme directly against Articles 9–15 — do not wait for harmonised standards. The high-risk obligations apply from 2 August 2026 regardless of standard availability.
    • When harmonised standards are published, map each standard precisely to the requirements it covers — the presumption applies only to the extent the standard addresses specific requirements, not as a blanket clearance.
    • Monitor for Article 41 common specifications — the Commission may publish these before harmonised standards are available, and they carry the same presumption effect.

    Common mistakes

    Assuming published CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 drafts or Technical Reports carry the Article 40 presumption is a common mistake — only standards whose references are published in the Official Journal create the presumption. Draft standards and pre-standards documents are reference material, not compliance shortcuts.

    Treating Article 40 as covering the full AI Act is a second mistake — the presumption applies to Chapter III Section 2 requirements only (Articles 9–15). The actor-obligation provisions (Articles 16–28) remain separately assessed regardless of standard compliance.

    Delaying compliance work until harmonised standards are published is a third mistake — the Article 9–15 obligations apply from 2 August 2026. Providers who waited for standards to be published before starting compliance work were non-compliant in the interim.

    Assuming a single harmonised standard covers all Articles 9–15 requirements is a fourth mistake — different standards will cover different requirement areas, and the presumption applies only to the specific requirements each standard addresses.

    Open questions

    • When the first AI Act harmonised standards will be published in the Official Journal — not confirmed on this research pass; the CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 timeline depends on the standardisation committee process and Commission review.
    • Whether the Commission will publish Article 41 common specifications before harmonised standards are available — the Commission has the power to do so; no common specifications had been published as of August 2026.
    • Whether the Digital Omnibus changed Article 40’s presumption mechanism or standardisation request scope — Article 40 carries the Omnibus disclaimer; the mechanism described here is pre-amendment.
    • Whether CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 Technical Specifications (as distinct from European Norms) can be OJ-referenced under Article 40 — Article 40(1) refers to ‘harmonised standards or parts thereof’; the exact relationship between Technical Specifications and the OJ-referencing process is not elaborated in the statute.

    Frequently asked

    FAQ
    Can I use CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 draft standards to demonstrate compliance today?
    Draft standards and pre-standards documents (Technical Reports, Technical Specifications) are useful as interpretive reference material for Articles 9–15 compliance work. They do not create the Article 40 presumption of conformity. To demonstrate compliance, you assess your system directly against Articles 9–15 using the statutory text as the standard, with JTC 21 documents as supporting interpretive guidance.
    FAQ
    Does Article 40 compliance replace conformity assessment under Article 43?
    No. Conformity assessment (Article 43) is a separate procedural step that determines which route the provider follows to demonstrate compliance — internal control assessment or notified body assessment. Article 40 affects what evidence satisfies the substantive requirements under that assessment; it does not eliminate the assessment step itself.
    FAQ
    What is the difference between a harmonised standard and a common specification?
    A harmonised standard is developed by an independent European standardisation body (CEN, CENELEC, ETSI) following a Commission standardisation request. A common specification is a Commission implementing act adopted directly. Both create a presumption of conformity when published. Common specifications can be adopted faster and are used when standards are absent, insufficient, or where specific safety concerns require a direct Commission response.
