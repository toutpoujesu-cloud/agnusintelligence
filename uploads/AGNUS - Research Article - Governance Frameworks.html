AI Governance

AI Governance Frameworks: The International Landscape

AI governance frameworks are structured sets of principles, policies, and accountability mechanisms that guide how organisations develop and deploy AI responsibly. The OECD AI Principles are the primary international reference, adopted by 42 member countries and embedded in the EU AI Act, the UK AI Safety Framework, and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. Understanding the landscape is the entry point to any compliance or governance programme.

AI governance is not a single framework. It is a layered landscape of international principles, regional regulation, national implementation, and voluntary commitments — each operating at a different level and with different legal force.

    The major frameworks compared

    Binding Law
    EU AI Act
    European Union · In force August 2024
    The most consequential binding AI governance framework for organisations operating in or selling to EU markets. Risk-based architecture: prohibited practices, high-risk AI, GPAI models, and general transparency obligations. Applies to providers and deployers regardless of where they are established if their system is used in the EU. Phased application: prohibited practices from February 2025, GPAI from August 2025, main body from August 2026 (with Omnibus extension to December 2027 for most Annex III categories). Covered in full across 24 AGNUS Research articles.
    Voluntary Technical Standard
    NIST AI Risk Management Framework
    National Institute of Standards and Technology (US) · January 2023
    The most operationally developed governance vocabulary for AI risk management. Four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. The companion AI RMF Playbook provides 72 suggested actions. Voluntary for most US organisations (no US omnibus AI law); referenced in federal procurement and sector-specific guidance. Many organisations use the NIST AI RMF as the internal governance architecture and map it to binding requirements (EU AI Act, sector rules) as the overlay.
    Certifiable Management System Standard
    ISO/IEC 42001:2023
    International Organization for Standardization · December 2023
    The first international standard for AI management systems. Follows the ISO management system structure (Plan-Do-Check-Act) used in ISO 27001 (information security) and ISO 9001 (quality). Certification is available from accredited certification bodies. Provides a documented, auditable record of AI governance processes. Not legally required except where sector-specific rules mandate it. Increasingly referenced in procurement requirements and due diligence frameworks.
    Principles-Based National Framework
    UK AI Safety Framework
    UK Government / AI Safety Institute · 2023–ongoing
    The UK has taken a principles-based approach without omnibus AI legislation. Five cross-sector principles: safety, security and robustness; transparency and explainability; fairness; accountability and governance; contestability and redress. Sector regulators apply the principles within their existing frameworks. The AI Safety Institute focuses on frontier model evaluation. The UK does not apply the EU AI Act (post-Brexit), but UK-based organisations that operate in the EU remain subject to it.
    G7 Political Commitment
    Hiroshima AI Process
    G7 · October 2023
    The G7 Hiroshima AI Process produced a set of guiding principles and a voluntary code of conduct for advanced AI developers. 11 principles covering transparency, safety, accountability, and international collaboration. Addressed primarily at frontier model developers. Voluntary commitment; no enforcement mechanism. Significant as an early multilateral signal on frontier AI governance expectations and as the basis for subsequent voluntary commitments by major AI developers.
    Binding Law (China)
    China AI Interim Measures
    Cyberspace Administration of China · August 2023
    China has taken a sectoral and incremental approach: interim measures on generative AI services (2023), separate measures on algorithmic recommendations (2022) and deep synthesis (deepfakes, 2022). Applies to organisations that provide AI services to the public within China. Key requirements: security assessment before deployment, labelling of AI-generated content, content restrictions. Does not apply to AI used internally or for research. Significant for any organisation providing AI services in the Chinese market.

    The five OECD principles in practice

    P1

    Inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being

    AI should benefit people and the planet. In practice: impact assessments that identify distributional effects, access considerations for underserved populations, and energy and environmental impact disclosure. The EU AI Act operationalises this through the fundamental rights impact assessment (Article 27) for public-sector deployers and through Annex III’s coverage of essential services (credit, housing, education) as high-risk use cases.

    P2

    Human-centred values, fairness and non-discrimination

    AI should respect human rights, democratic values and diversity. In practice: bias testing and fairness metrics, protected characteristic analysis, contestability mechanisms. Operationalised in the EU AI Act through Article 10 data governance requirements (representative datasets, bias monitoring), Article 13 transparency (right to explanation), and Article 14 human oversight.

    P3

    Transparency and explainability

    People should understand AI-based outcomes and be able to challenge them. In practice: logging and audit trails, explanation mechanisms, public disclosure of AI system use. Operationalised in the EU AI Act through Article 12 (logging), Article 13 (transparency and provision of information to deployers), and Article 50 (transparency obligations for certain AI systems toward end users, including the right to know when interacting with an AI).

    P4

    Robustness, security and safety

    AI systems must be robust and safe throughout their entire lifecycle. In practice: adversarial testing, red-teaming, vulnerability management, post-market monitoring. Operationalised in the EU AI Act through Article 15 (accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity), Article 72 (post-market monitoring), and Article 55 (adversarial testing for GPAI models with systemic risk).

    P5

    Accountability

    Organisations should be accountable for AI systems functioning in line with the principles. In practice: clear allocation of responsibility across the supply chain, incident reporting, governance structures, fines for non-compliance. Operationalised in the EU AI Act through the actor-role framework (Article 3 definitions of provider/deployer/importer/distributor), Article 16 (provider obligations), Article 26 (deployer obligations), and Article 99 (penalties).

    How the landscape is organised

    The EU AI Act as anchor framework

    What organizations must do

    • Map your AI systems against the applicable frameworks by jurisdiction: identify which binding laws apply (EU AI Act for EU market exposure, China interim measures for China market, sector-specific rules for regulated industries), then layer voluntary frameworks (NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001) as the operational architecture.
    • Classify your AI systems by risk level before designing controls — classification determines what governance is proportionate. Use the EU AI Act risk taxonomy if EU-exposed; use the NIST AI RMF impact categories for all systems.
    • Assign clear accountability for each AI system in your inventory — identify whether your organisation is acting as provider, deployer, importer, or distributor under the EU AI Act, and assign corresponding obligations to specific roles or functions.
    • Consider ISO/IEC 42001 certification as the audit trail for your AI governance programme — it provides a documented, auditable record that satisfies due diligence requirements and increasingly appears in procurement requirements.
    • Track the OECD AI Policy Observatory for national implementation developments in your operating jurisdictions — the Observatory monitors how OECD member countries are implementing the Principles and flags new binding requirements as they emerge.

    Common mistakes

    Treating OECD Principles adherence as compliance with the EU AI Act is a common mistake — the Principles are a political commitment with no legal force. EU AI Act compliance requires satisfying specific legal requirements by risk category and actor role. The Principles are a useful diagnostic, not a compliance test.

    Building a governance programme around voluntary frameworks alone is a second mistake — organisations with EU market exposure are subject to binding law. A NIST AI RMF-aligned programme that does not address EU AI Act requirements is not a compliance programme for EU-exposed AI systems.

    Failing to account for extraterritorial reach is a third mistake — the EU AI Act, like the GDPR, applies based on where the system is used, not where the provider is established. A US-based provider whose AI system is used in the EU is subject to the Act regardless of where the organisation is headquartered.

    Waiting for a single comprehensive national framework is a fourth mistake — the US, UK, and most other jurisdictions are using sector-specific and incremental approaches. An organisation operating in multiple jurisdictions must manage a patchwork of frameworks, not wait for harmonisation.

    Open questions

    • Whether the US will adopt omnibus AI legislation at federal level — as of August 2026, no comprehensive federal AI law has been enacted; governance relies on executive action, agency guidance, and voluntary commitments.
    • How the G7 Hiroshima voluntary code of conduct has been adopted by major AI developers — initial commitments were made in late 2023; implementation monitoring is ongoing but not systematically published.
    • Whether ISO/IEC 42001 certification will become a mandatory requirement in regulated sector procurement — currently referenced voluntarily; sector regulators (financial services, healthcare) may begin requiring it as AI governance expectations mature.
    • How the OECD AI Principles will be updated as generative AI and agentic AI systems create governance gaps not addressed in the 2024 revision — the OECD AI Policy Observatory is monitoring; further revision likely.

    Frequently asked

    FAQ
    Where should an organisation start when building an AI governance programme?
    Start with inventory and classification. List every AI system in use or development. Classify each by risk level using the EU AI Act taxonomy if you have EU market exposure, or the NIST AI RMF impact categories if not. Classification tells you what level of governance is proportionate. Then assign accountability (who is the provider, who is the deployer for each system), identify the binding legal requirements that apply, and layer the operational framework (NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001) on top as the implementation architecture.
    FAQ
    Is ISO/IEC 42001 certification a substitute for EU AI Act compliance?
    No. ISO/IEC 42001 is a management system standard — it certifies that your governance processes are documented and systematic. It does not certify that you have met the specific technical and procedural requirements of the EU AI Act. Certification provides a useful audit trail and demonstrates governance maturity, but it must be accompanied by substantive compliance with the Act’s requirements for each AI system in scope.
    FAQ
    Which framework should a small organisation with no EU exposure prioritise?
    NIST AI RMF as the operational architecture, with ISO/IEC 42001 if the organisation wants a certifiable record. The OECD Principles are useful as a diagnostic check. Sector-specific requirements (FDA AI/ML guidance for medical devices, FFIEC guidance for financial services, etc.) take priority if they apply. The EU AI Act does not apply unless the system is used in the EU — but as the Act’s reach is broad and many supply chains have EU exposure, checking applicability is worthwhile regardless of headquarters location.
