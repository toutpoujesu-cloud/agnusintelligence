ISO 42001: The AI Management System Standard — What It Requires and How to Get Certified

Published 11 Sep 2026
Reading time 15 min
Pillar AI Governance
Source ISO/IEC 42001:2023 · EU AI Act Art. 40

ISO 42001 is the first international standard for AI management systems. Published in December 2023, it provides a framework for organisations to establish, implement, maintain, and continually improve an AI management system — a structured approach to governing AI across its entire lifecycle. Unlike many AI governance frameworks, ISO 42001 is auditable and certifiable. An organisation can seek third-party certification that its AI management system meets the standard.

What ISO 42001 is and what it is not

ISO 42001 is a management system standard — which means it specifies what an organisation must do, not how it must do it. Like ISO 9001 (quality management) and ISO 27001 (information security management), it requires organisations to establish policies, assign responsibilities, assess risks, implement controls, monitor performance, and drive continual improvement. It does not specify technical requirements for individual AI systems — that is the role of technical standards such as ISO/IEC 23894 (AI risk management) and ISO/IEC 22989 (AI concepts and terminology).

The standard applies to any organisation involved in developing, providing, or using AI systems — regardless of size, sector, or the nature of the AI. A multinational technology company developing foundation models and a mid-sized financial services firm using a third-party AI for credit screening can both be certified to ISO 42001. The specific controls implemented will differ significantly — the standard requires proportionality — but the management system structure is the same.

The structure: ten clauses

ISO 42001 follows the High-Level Structure common to all modern ISO management system standards, which means it shares a common architecture with ISO 9001, ISO 27001, and ISO 14001. Organisations already certified to any of these standards will find the structure familiar. The ten clauses are:

ClauseTitleKey requirements
4Context of the organisationIdentify internal and external issues, interested parties, and the scope of the AI management system. Understand the organisation's role (developer, provider, deployer, or combination) in the AI value chain.
5LeadershipTop management must demonstrate leadership and commitment. An AI policy must be established, communicated, and maintained. Roles, responsibilities, and authorities must be assigned.
6PlanningAddress risks and opportunities. Set AI objectives. Plan how to achieve them. This clause is where the AI risk management process is established — informed by ISO/IEC 23894.
7SupportResources, competence, awareness, communication, and documented information. AI literacy is explicitly addressed: personnel whose work affects AI performance must be competent in relevant areas.
8OperationThe core operational requirements: AI system impact assessment, AI system design and development processes, data management, documentation, and supplier management for AI. This clause contains the majority of the substantive requirements.
9Performance evaluationMonitoring, measurement, analysis, and evaluation. Internal audit requirements. Management review — at defined intervals, top management must review the AI management system's performance.
10ImprovementNonconformity and corrective action. Continual improvement — the AI management system must improve over time, not remain static.

Clause 8 in depth: the operational requirements

Clause 8 contains the requirements that most directly translate into day-to-day AI governance practice. It is where ISO 42001 is most distinct from other management system standards.

8.2 — AI system impact assessment

Before implementing an AI system, the organisation must assess its potential impacts — on individuals, on society, and on the environment. This is ISO 42001's equivalent of the EU AI Act's FRIA. The assessment must consider: the nature and purpose of the AI system; the people who will use it and those who will be affected by it; the potential for harm; and whether and how the harm could be mitigated. The assessment must be documented and must inform the decisions made about whether and how to deploy the system.

8.4 — AI system lifecycle processes

The standard requires documented processes for managing AI systems throughout their lifecycle — from initial concept and design through development, deployment, operation, and decommissioning. This clause is where the AI inventory requirement emerges: organisations cannot manage AI system lifecycles without knowing what systems they operate. The lifecycle processes must address: data acquisition and management; system design and development; verification and validation; and handover to operational use.

8.5 — Data for AI systems

ISO 42001 devotes a dedicated sub-clause to data management for AI systems, reflecting the centrality of data quality to AI system quality. Requirements include: that data used to develop and operate AI systems is appropriate for its intended purpose; that data governance practices are established; that data lineage is understood and documented; and that the potential for bias introduced through data is assessed and mitigated.

8.6 — Information for interested parties

The organisation must provide appropriate information about its AI systems to those who need it — including system users, those affected by AI decisions, and regulators. This clause aligns directly with the EU AI Act's transparency requirements (Art. 13) for high-risk AI systems.

The relationship between ISO 42001 and the EU AI Act

The European Commission has indicated that compliance with harmonised standards under the EU AI Act (Article 40) will create a presumption of conformity with the corresponding requirements. ISO 42001 is a candidate for harmonisation — if it is formally harmonised, certification to ISO 42001 would create a presumption of conformity with the organisational and management requirements of the EU AI Act for the scope covered.

At the time of writing, harmonisation is not yet confirmed. Organisations pursuing ISO 42001 certification for EU AI Act purposes should not assume it will be sufficient to discharge all EU AI Act obligations — particularly the technical requirements for high-risk AI (documentation, accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity) which go beyond what a management system standard covers.

The overlap is nonetheless significant. ISO 42001's Clause 8 requirements for AI system impact assessment, lifecycle management, and data governance map closely to the EU AI Act's requirements for high-risk AI systems. An organisation with a mature ISO 42001 implementation will be substantially better positioned to demonstrate EU AI Act compliance than one without.

The certification pathway

Stage 1 — Gap analysis. Assess the organisation's current AI governance practices against the requirements of ISO 42001. Identify what is in place, what is partially in place, and what is absent. The gap analysis should produce a prioritised remediation plan with realistic timelines.

Stage 2 — Implementation. Build or strengthen the management system elements required by the standard. For most organisations, this means: establishing an AI policy signed by top management; defining the scope of the AI management system; building or formalising the AI inventory; establishing the AI risk assessment process; documenting lifecycle processes; and building the monitoring and measurement programme. Implementation typically takes 6 to 18 months depending on the organisation's starting position and the complexity of its AI portfolio.

Stage 3 — Internal audit. Before seeking external certification, the organisation must conduct at least one full internal audit of the AI management system — assessing whether the implemented system meets the standard's requirements and is functioning as intended. The internal audit must be conducted by competent auditors who are independent of the areas being audited.

Stage 4 — External certification audit. A certification body accredited to audit against ISO 42001 conducts the external audit. The audit typically consists of a Stage 1 documentation review and a Stage 2 on-site implementation audit. If conformity is demonstrated, the certification body issues a certificate valid for three years, subject to annual surveillance audits.

What certification is and is not. ISO 42001 certification confirms that the organisation has an AI management system that meets the standard's requirements. It does not certify that every individual AI system the organisation operates is safe, fair, or compliant with the EU AI Act. It does not guarantee that AI systems will not cause harm. It demonstrates that the organisation has a structured, documented, and audited approach to managing AI — which is a meaningful and verifiable governance statement, but not a blanket conformity claim.

Which organisations should pursue certification

ISO 42001 certification is most valuable for: organisations that develop or provide AI systems to other businesses or the public, where certification differentiates them in procurement processes; financial services organisations, healthcare providers, and other regulated entities where evidence of structured AI governance is increasingly expected by regulators; and organisations preparing for EU AI Act compliance, where ISO 42001 provides a structured framework and may in future carry harmonisation benefits. For organisations that only use AI systems procured from third parties, self-assessment against ISO 42001 (without formal certification) may be proportionate and sufficient for most governance purposes.
