AGNUS Academy Level 8 · Build an Application L8-09 — The Shaping
Step 1 of 6 — Understand4 min

The Blueprint said what the app does. The Shaping decides how it behaves, feels, and appeals to its actual audience.

Claude here is a research partner and design collaborator — not just a code generator. Design starts with understanding the audience, not with an aesthetic.

Why this matters

"Design is a sort of problem solving that starts with an understanding of the audience. It means finding elegant solutions to complex (and often conflicting) user needs, mental states, and abilities."

Two parallel tracks

Track 1 — Behavior & Workflow

What the application does, how it flows, how it responds. Claude defines the user journey, system behavior, edge cases.

Track 2 — UX & Aesthetics

How it looks, feels, and appeals to its audience. Claude researches the audience, suggests aesthetics, refines interactions.

Step 2 of 6 — Track 1: Behavior6 min

Research before behavior — understand the user first

Before Claude can suggest how the application should behave, it needs to understand the user:

Before we design the behavior of this application, I need you to research the target audience. Who are they? What are their goals with this interface? What are they familiar with? In what context is this design appearing? What are their pain points and mental models?

Step 3 of 6 — Track 2: Aesthetics6 min

The aesthetic must be audience-driven, not arbitrary

"AI design tools can be aesthetically pleasing, but often they're not actually 'design'. ...Without any background, these tools are happy to output something with many of the common signals of 'good' design, but beneath the surface they are very limited."
Based on the research you conducted, recommend an aesthetic direction for this application. Consider: The audience's age, cultural background, and technological familiarity The context in which they'll use the application (office, mobile, on-the-go) Their goals and emotional state when using the application The industry and competitive landscape Brand personality and values Explain your reasoning and provide specific recommendations for: color palette, typography, layout density, visual hierarchy, and emotional tone.
"If you don't need to cater your product to human factors, technical constraints, or other limitations, then what you needed wasn't really 'design' in the first place." — aesthetics are in service of user needs, not decoration.

Step 4 of 6 — The Claude Aesthetic Trap6 min

Homogenization is a named, real problem

"The classic AI-summarized document, complete with bullet points, sub-points and that ubiquitous colored vertical line... They all look exactly the same. The fonts are microscopic. The writing is flavorless... I'm calling this the 'Claude Aesthetic'."
"The output is often visually polished, but it remains structurally shallow... It enables teams to generate a high volume of visually consistent but structurally similar layouts."

You must learn to recognize when you're accepting a "Claude default" rather than making an intentional design decision. The aesthetic must serve the audience, not the AI's default patterns.

The counter-strategy

ProblemSolution
Visual HomogeneityDeliberately prompt for audience-specific aesthetics: "What would a [specific cultural group/age group/industry] find appealing and trustworthy?"
Structural ShallownessPush for depth: "What happens in edge cases?" "How does this scale?" "What would a power user want that a novice wouldn't?"
Default TypographySpecify: "For a professional audience, recommend a typography system that conveys authority and precision."
Unshaped default
  • Generic rounded cards, uniform spacing
  • No research into who will actually use it
  • Copied from the most common pattern for "this kind of app"
Actually shaped
  • Layout decisions justified by a stated audience need
  • Workflow matches how this specific user actually works
  • Deliberately un-generic where the product calls for it

Step 6 of 6 — Practice & Pass5 min

A builder asks Claude Code to "make it look nice" with no further detail, for an app used by elderly patients managing medication reminders. What's the risk?

AThe default aesthetic may not suit an elderly, possibly low-vision audience at all — no research into the actual audience ever happened, so the mismatch was never caught.
BThere is no risk — a well-trained assistant always produces the ideal design regardless of context.
CThe only risk is a slightly longer build time.
D"Make it look nice" is already specific enough instruction for any audience.

New scenario — no hints

A team building an internal tool for warehouse staff, who wear gloves and check the app on shared tablets, tells Claude Code: "Large touch targets, high contrast, minimal typing — most actions should be single-tap." What did they just do correctly, per the two-track model and the counter-strategy?

AThey named a specific, real audience constraint (gloves, shared tablets) and translated it into concrete behavior and aesthetic decisions — exactly the counter-strategy to the Claude Aesthetic trap.
BNothing meaningful — any UI instruction is equally generic.
CThey wrote a Blueprint, not a Shaping instruction.
DThis instruction only affects Track 2 aesthetics, not Track 1 behavior.
ELarge touch targets are always the wrong choice for internal tools.
