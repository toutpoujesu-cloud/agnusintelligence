Output validation is not
optional for consequential work.

An agent that produces a result is not the same as an agent that produces a correct result. Validation is the structured step between receiving output and acting on it. Without it, you are acting on trust rather than evidence.

6 steps ~14 min Prerequisite: M12

Why validation is a discipline, not a check

Validation is easy to skip when output looks right. It looks professional, it's well-formatted, the numbers seem plausible. That's exactly when validation matters most — because confident-looking incorrect output causes more harm than output that obviously looks wrong.

Validation is not re-reading the output once. It is applying a structured set of checks calibrated to the output type and the risk of being wrong. Different outputs require different validation depth.

Next: the three validation layers
Step 2 of 6Three validation layers
4 min
Layer 1
Completeness
Did the output cover everything it was supposed to? Are any required sections missing? Does the output match the scope of the task?
Did I get what I asked for?
Layer 2
Internal consistency
Do the parts of the output agree with each other? Do the numbers add up? Does the conclusion follow from the data presented? Are there contradictions within the output itself?
Does it make sense internally?
Layer 3
External verification
Do specific factual claims match their primary sources? Do the figures match the source data? Are named references real? This is the deepest layer — applied selectively to high-stakes specific claims.
Is it actually true?

Not every output needs all three layers

A structural output — "here are five options for the meeting agenda" — needs Layer 1 (are there five options?) and Layer 2 (do they make sense?). Layer 3 (verify each against a source) is unnecessary. A legal citation in a compliance brief needs all three layers, with Layer 3 applied to every specific claim. Calibrate to the risk of being wrong.

Next: a worked example
Step 4 of 6A worked validation example
3 min
Agent output — financial summary for board pack
Q3 2026 Financial Summary
Total revenue: €2,840,000
Total costs: €1,920,000
Operating profit: €940,000 (33.1% margin)
Cash balance: €1,240,000
Headcount: 142
Revenue per employee: €20,000
✓
Layer 1 — Completeness: All required fields present (revenue, costs, profit, cash, headcount).
✗
Layer 2 — Consistency failure: €2,840,000 / 142 employees = €20,000 revenue per employee. But if that's monthly revenue, the annual figure would be €240,000 per employee — which seems low for this business. And if that's annual revenue, €20,000/employee is unusually low. Flag for clarification.
✗
Layer 2 — Arithmetic check: €2,840,000 − €1,920,000 = €920,000. The output states €940,000. Discrepancy of €20,000. Verify the source data.
→
Layer 3 — External verify: Verify total revenue against the actual Q3 revenue records. Verify headcount against HR system. Do not present these figures to the board before both checks are complete.
The arithmetic check caught a €20,000 discrepancy. That's a Layer 2 check — no external source needed, just arithmetic. These are the fastest validation steps and they catch a significant proportion of errors. Always do the numbers before you go deeper.

Step 5 of 6Practice
3 min

An agent produces a supplier payment summary: "Processed 12 invoices. Total paid: €18,400. Largest single payment: €4,200 to Oslo Tech Partners." You check: 12 invoices × average €1,533 = €18,396, close enough. The largest payment is €4,200 which fits within the reported total. You approve the summary for filing.

Which validation layer is missing from this review?
A
Layer 1 — the review didn't confirm all 12 invoice numbers were listed.
B
Layer 3 — the review confirmed internal consistency (arithmetic checks out) but didn't verify the figures against the actual payment records. Whether 12 payments were made, whether the amounts match, and whether Oslo Tech was the correct payee all require checking against the system of record — not just the agent's summary.
C
Layer 2 — the arithmetic was not verified accurately enough.

Step 6 of 6Pass — hidden test
3 min

A colleague argues: "Validation is important for high-stakes work, but for routine tasks we should just trust the agent's output — otherwise we're defeating the purpose of automation." An operator responds: "Validation doesn't mean checking everything manually — it means applying the right checks at the right depth for each output type."

Who is right, and what is the key insight?
A
The colleague is right — over-validation destroys the efficiency gains from automation.
B
The operator is right. Validation is not "check everything manually" — that would defeat automation. It is "apply the right depth of checks for this output type and risk level." A routine order status update needs Layer 1 only (did the update apply?). A financial summary needs all three layers. Calibrated validation preserves automation efficiency while maintaining appropriate governance.
C
Both are partially right — the solution is to validate only a random sample of routine outputs.
