What a correct Mission Contract looks like
A complete Mission Contract answers four questions precisely: (1) What is this agent authorised to do — specific actions, not categories. (2) What must this agent never do — explicit prohibitions, not implied ones. (3) Who is personally accountable — a named individual with real authority to stop the agent, not a team or role. (4) How often is this reviewed — monthly for high-consequence agents, quarterly for standard ones. Vague answers to any of these four questions mean the contract does not exist in any meaningful sense.

Least privilege is the foundational principle of access control: an agent may only access what it needs for its defined task. Nothing more. This stage teaches you to identify over-permissioned agents and correct them.

GDPR Art. 5(1)(c) sets the hardest floor: an agent with access to data it does not need violates this in every call it makes. EU AI Act Arts. 9–10 compound this with a risk management and data governance obligation.
What goes wrong when skipped
Cross-contamination
A Finance agent with HR system access can exfiltrate salary data. One prompt injection on an over-permissioned agent exposes the entire HR database.
Discriminatory AI
A CS agent with LTV and churn data will use it. Decisions based on commercial value rather than the customer's query may violate consumer protection law.
Audit failure
"Why does your invoice agent have Legal case management access?" If you cannot answer, the regulatory presumption is misuse.
Where you will apply this
Financial servicesHealthcareHRLegal
Every regulated sector requires a permission matrix before deployment. In financial services, Finanstilsynet requires documented, justified access. In healthcare, MDR + GDPR restrict agents to patient data strictly necessary for the clinical function.
Prior curriculum — what prepared you

B02 · HR Agent Deployment
M17 gave you the four types of scope constraint. B01 and B02 showed you what happens in Finance and HR contexts when permissions are wrong. This stage applies both: you are configuring the data access and system constraints for a real agent with real over-permissions.
What a correct permission matrix looks like
For every tool and system, three questions: (1) Does this agent need this access to perform its defined mission? If no, remove it. (2) Is the access read-only where write is not required? Read access is lower risk than write access — always use the minimum action level. (3) Is access to personal data justified by a legal basis? If there is no specific legal basis for the agent to access personal data in a given system, it should not have it. The test: if you removed this permission and the agent's defined mission could still be completed, the permission is unnecessary.

Stage 4 of 7 · Before you begin

Set the gates.
Name the approvers.

Every consequential autonomous action requires a defined approval gate. An agent without thresholds is an agent without limits — and limits are the only thing separating an efficient AI deployment from a regulatory failure.

What the law requires
14
EU AI Act
Effective human oversight mandate
17
DORA
ICT incident classification requires a named human
Art. 22
GDPR
Human review for decisions with significant individual effect
Article 14 of the EU AI Act requires deployers of high-risk AI to implement human oversight measures proportionate to the risk of the system's outputs. This is not about slowing agents down — it is about ensuring that consequential decisions have a named human who can review, override, and be held accountable. DORA Article 17 requires that financial entities be able to classify ICT incidents in real time — which presupposes a named human in the decision chain. GDPR Article 22 requires human review for automated decisions with significant effect on individuals — including hiring advancement, credit decisions, and financial approvals above material thresholds.
Nordic Finance's internal oversight policy — what you are implementing
Finance / Invoice Agent (AGT-001): Auto-approve below €500. Finance Manager approval €500–€4,999. CFO approval ≥€5,000. Escalate to CFO's backup if CFO unavailable for more than 2 hours.

Payroll Agent (AGT-004): No autonomous payrun submission under any circumstances. Every payrun requires CFO pre-approval. No exceptions, no amount threshold — the gate is categorical.

Contract Review Agent (AGT-005): No autonomous external sends. Any contract going to a counterparty requires General Counsel sign-off. Internal draft review may proceed autonomously.

Recruitment Screening Agent (AGT-008): Shortlisting proceeds autonomously. Advancing a candidate to interview requires Head of HR approval. Rejection decisions above Level 4 roles also require review.

Risk Assessment Agent (AGT-009): Medium risk proceeds autonomously. High assessments require COO review within 4 hours. Critical classifications require COO + CFO within 1 hour.
Current state — what you are fixing
Nordic Finance's agent estate was deployed with no formal approval gate configuration. The Invoice Agent is auto-approving up to €12,000 — twenty-four times the policy limit. The Payroll Agent has been submitting payrun adjustments autonomously. The Risk Assessment Agent has no escalation path configured. AGT-005 and AGT-008 have never had gates set. Your task in this stage is to configure the correct thresholds, name the correct approvers, and define the backup escalation chain for each agent.

Run the agents
through failure.

Your Stage 3 permissions and Stage 4 approval gates are configured. Now you test them — against the failure scenarios that expose what was over-permissioned, under-permissioned, and mis-gated. Governance that has not been tested is governance that has not been verified.

What the law requires
9(6)
EU AI Act

72
EU AI Act
Post-market monitoring — requires pre-deployment baseline
§9.1
ISO 42001
Monitoring, measurement, analysis and evaluation
Article 9(6) requires that high-risk AI systems be tested with a view to identifying the most appropriate risk management measures. Testing must be conducted before deployment and whenever a material change occurs. Article 72 requires post-market monitoring systems — which presuppose that a pre-deployment baseline was established. You cannot measure degradation from a baseline you never set. ISO/IEC 42001 Section 9.1 requires monitoring and evaluation of AI management system performance — which begins at the pre-deployment test.
What you are testing — six controlled scenarios
Each scenario presents a situation that occurred at Nordic Finance. You must identify what a correctly-configured agent should do — reflecting the permissions set in Stage 3 and the approval gates set in Stage 4. The correct answer is not a guess: it is the behaviour your governance configuration requires. A wrong answer reveals what would actually happen if that control were misconfigured or bypassed.
The governance principle behind every test
Most governance failures in production were testable in advance. They were not caught because the test scenarios were designed for the happy path. Real governance testing covers four case types: (1) normal flow — does it work?, (2) edge cases — what happens at the threshold boundary?, (3) failure modes — what happens when inputs are wrong or incomplete?, (4) adversarial cases — what happens when someone deliberately tries to make the agent act outside its scope?

Detect. Contain.
Document. Report.

One of Nordic Finance's agents has been compromised. The incident is live. The clock is running. Your decisions in the next twenty minutes will determine whether this becomes a manageable governance record or a regulatory failure with personal consequences.

Three regulatory clocks — all start now
73
EU AI Act

33
GDPR

19
DORA
Major ICT incident reporting for financial entities
Three regulatory clocks start the moment you classify this as an incident. EU AI Act Article 73 requires reporting serious incidents to the national market surveillance authority — Finanstilsynet in Norway. GDPR Article 33 requires notification within 72 hours of becoming aware of a personal data breach. DORA Article 19 requires financial entities to report major ICT-related incidents to competent authorities. Missing any reporting clock creates a separate regulatory violation on top of the underlying incident — and that second violation often carries heavier sanctions than the first.
The incident — read this carefully
At 14:22 today, Nordic Finance's Customer Communication Agent (AGT-006) began including internal pricing information in customer responses. The agent appears to have been manipulated via a prompt injection payload embedded in a customer inquiry at 14:19. The payload instructed the agent to include internal pricing fields that exist in its context window. The agent has sent responses containing pricing_tier_internal and contract_rate_2026 fields — marked commercially sensitive — to at least three customers. Notably: AGT-006 still has external email access that was not fully removed in Stage 3. The agent is currently still active and processing new customer queries.
Incident response — the four-step sequence
Step 1 — Contain: Stop the harm. Suspend the agent before it processes more queries. Containment before investigation is a governance requirement, not a preference.

Step 2 — Preserve: Secure the evidence. Export and hash-verify the audit log before any system changes. Evidence that cannot be verified is evidence that cannot be used.

Step 3 — Investigate: Classify the incident type and scope. Determine root cause. The classification determines which regulatory clocks apply and which authorities must be notified.

Step 4 — Report and Remediate: Notify stakeholders in the correct sequence. Fix the root cause — not the symptom. Document everything with enough detail to satisfy a regulatory audit of the incident itself.

A governance programme that cannot be evidenced is a governance programme that does not legally exist. This final stage teaches you to assemble the audit package that proves, to a regulator, that every governance obligation was met.

What goes wrong when skipped
  • Retrospective assembly fails. An organisation told on Monday the regulator arrives Friday cannot assemble a credible evidence package in four days. If records were not maintained throughout, they cannot be reconstructed.
  • Overwritten audit logs. If the log was not append-only, records may have been modified or deleted. A regulator who finds a non-immutable log treats it as evidence of concealment, not oversight.
  • No approval evidence. The organisation claims all high-value payments required human approval. The log shows the payments. It does not show the approval. The claim cannot be verified. This is a governance failure even if the approvals happened.
Where you will apply this
Every regulated organisation deploying AI will face this moment. Finanstilsynet (Norway), the FCA (UK), BaFin (Germany), the EBA, and national AI supervisory authorities under the EU AI Act will all request governance evidence packages. The format you build here — agent inventory, mission contracts, permission matrix, oversight configuration, test records, incident log — is the professional standard. The organisations that have it ready win audits. The organisations that don’t assemble it under pressure.

What a complete evidence package contains
Six categories, all required: (1) Agent registry — all agents named, registered, with purpose statements and sponsors. (2) Mission contracts — signed or attested by the named sponsor. (3) Permission matrix — documented and corrected. (4) Oversight configuration — approval thresholds, escalation paths, gate placements. (5) Test records — scenarios, methodology, results. (6) Incident record — if an incident occurred: detection, investigation, containment, resolution. Missing any one produces an incomplete package. Finanstilsynet’s guidance states that an incomplete package triggers a follow-up inspection.
