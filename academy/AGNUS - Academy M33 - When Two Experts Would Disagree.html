Some outputs aren't wrong.
They're contested.

Verification against primary sources works for factual claims. But some questions don't have a single correct answer — they have legitimate expert disagreement. An AI that presents one side of a contested question as established fact is producing output that is more dangerous than a simple error.

6 steps ~15 min Prerequisite: M32

The contested claim problem

Verification modules M30–M32 dealt with claims that are either correct or incorrect — verifiable against primary sources. This module deals with a different category: claims where qualified experts genuinely disagree, where the evidence is mixed, or where the answer depends on which framework you apply.

An AI system presented with a contested question will typically produce a confident answer reflecting the most common position in its training data. It won't present the debate. It won't signal that experts disagree. It will produce a well-written, apparently authoritative answer to a question that doesn't have one.

Step 2 of 6What contested looks like — three examples
4 min
Expert A position
AI hiring tools reduce bias
When calibrated correctly, AI tools remove human cognitive bias from the initial screening phase, producing a larger and more diverse candidate pool.
Evidence: Amazon Rekognition study showed demographic parity improvements in screening. Pymetrics research, 2022.
Expert B position
AI hiring tools amplify bias
AI tools trained on historical hiring data replicate the biases of the humans who made those decisions — and apply them at scale, faster than human review could catch.
Evidence: Amazon's internal tool abandoned in 2018 after systematic bias against women. MIT Media Lab studies on facial recognition demographic gaps.
Expert A position
AI-generated code is production-ready with review
With proper human review, AI-generated code is as reliable as developer-written code and significantly faster to produce. Review catches the edge cases the AI misses.
Evidence: GitHub Copilot productivity studies showing 55% faster task completion.
Expert B position
AI-generated code introduces security vulnerabilities at scale
AI models confidently generate insecure code patterns — SQL injection vulnerabilities, improper authentication — that developers miss in review because the code looks structurally correct.
Evidence: Stanford study finding 40% of GitHub Copilot suggestions contained security vulnerabilities.

Why AI produces one position, not both

When an AI model is asked "do AI hiring tools reduce bias?", it will produce a response that reflects the most common position in its training data. If the training data contains more positive coverage than negative, it will produce a positive answer. If the debate is roughly balanced in its training data, it may hedge — but it won't clearly label the output as "this is a contested question."

The operator's responsibility: recognise contested questions, flag them as such when the AI doesn't, and ensure the output includes the debate — not just one side of it.

Four signals that a question is contested

These signals indicate that an AI output requires not just source verification, but a check for whether it presents both sides of the debate.

Signal 1 — The question involves prediction or projection
Any claim about future outcomes — will AI create or destroy more jobs? Will autonomous vehicles be safer than human drivers by 2030? — is inherently contested. Experts can model different futures from the same data.
Signal 2 — The question involves policy or ethics
Should AI-generated content be labelled? Should facial recognition be used by law enforcement? These depend on value judgments, not just evidence. An AI answer reflects whose values dominated the training data.
Signal 3 — The evidence points in multiple directions
Some studies show effect X; other equally rigorous studies show the opposite. The AI will likely report the majority finding without noting the minority studies.
Signal 4 — The answer changes by jurisdiction or regulatory framework
What AI systems are "high risk" depends on which framework you apply. The EU AI Act, UK AI regulation, and US executive orders produce different answers for the same system. An AI may give one jurisdiction's answer without noting the others.

Step 4 of 6How to handle contested output
3 min
Resolution framework — when AI output may be one side of a debate
1
Identify the signal
Does the output involve prediction, policy, mixed evidence, or jurisdiction variance? If yes, treat it as potentially contested.
2
Check for the opposing view
Ask the AI: "What is the strongest opposing argument to the position above?" A well-calibrated model will generate it. Compare both sides for evidence quality.
3
Find the primary sources for both positions
Apply M30 verification to both sides: named source, publication date, exact figure, context preserved. A "side" that has no primary sources is a position, not evidence.
4
Label the output appropriately
If the question is contested, the output must say so: "There is ongoing expert debate on this question. The main positions are..." Not "the answer is X" followed by one set of citations.
5
Escalate if the decision depends on it
If a consequential business decision rests on a contested question, don't resolve it with AI output. Escalate to a qualified expert who can assess the evidence and take accountability for the position.
The honest output

"This is a contested question."

An AI that says "there is ongoing expert debate on this question, and here are the main positions with their evidence" is producing better output than one that confidently states one side. Training yourself to recognise contested questions — and to ask the AI for the opposing view — is one of the most valuable Level 5 skills.

Step 5 of 6Practice
3 min

A strategy team asks an AI agent: "Should we implement a four-day work week to improve productivity?" The agent responds with a confident 400-word analysis arguing that the four-day work week improves productivity, citing the Iceland trial (2015–2019) and Microsoft Japan study (2019). A team member says: "This reads as settled — we could use this for the board recommendation."

What is the problem with using this output as-is for a board recommendation?
A
The citations need to be verified — the Iceland and Microsoft Japan studies may not actually support the conclusion.
B
The output presents one side of a contested policy and evidence question as "settled." The four-day work week has conflicting evidence — sectors with knowledge work show different outcomes from manufacturing or healthcare; studies differ in methodology; cultural context varies. The board recommendation should present the debate, the conditions under which productivity gains have been found, and the conditions under which they haven't — not a confident single-position recommendation.
C
The output is too long — a board recommendation should be shorter and more direct.

Step 6 of 6Pass — hidden test
3 min

A compliance officer asks an AI: "Is our AI recruitment tool classified as high-risk under applicable law?" The AI answers: "Yes, AI systems used in recruitment and employment decisions are classified as high-risk under the EU AI Act (Annex III, point 4(a))." This is factually correct. However, the company also operates in the UK, where the AI regulation framework is different, and in the US, where classification is state-level and varies. The compliance officer marks the question as resolved.

What has the compliance officer missed?
A
Nothing — the EU AI Act answer is correct and the classification is settled.
B
Signal 4 — the answer changes by jurisdiction. The EU AI Act answer is correct for EU operations. But "high-risk under applicable law" for a company operating in EU, UK, and US requires three separate answers under three different frameworks. The AI answered one jurisdiction's question completely and accurately — but the compliance question covers multiple jurisdictions. Marking it as "resolved" based on the EU answer leaves UK and US obligations unaddressed.
C
The AI's answer needs to be verified against the actual EU AI Act text before it can be used.
