AGNUS Academy Level 8 · Build an Application L8-08 — The Blueprint
Step 1 of 6 — Understand4 min

Design first, code second — the Blueprint is the architecture, not a to-do list.

Before a single line of code is written, you capture your vision and transform it into a structured, executable blueprint. Rushing to code before defining the architecture is the primary cause of project failure.

Why this matters — the core philosophy

The blueprint is the birth of the application's architecture. As one industry expert puts it:

"A design-first approach: the contract is primary, the implementation is secondary."

This phase instills that discipline directly — you learn that the blueprint isn't a formality before "the real work," it is the real work of designing an application, done before any code exists.

The Design-First workflow — four steps, two drivers

1Vision Capture — you articulate the idea, workflow, and goalsStudent
2Blueprint Generation — Claude structures the vision, asks clarifying questions, produces a planClaude
3Refinement — you review, ask "what are the open questions?" to surface gapsBoth
4Final Plan — the blueprint is complete and ready for executionBoth

The example we'll follow through this module

Raw vision — the unstructured starting point

"I want to build a customer support agent for a small e-commerce store. When a customer submits a ticket, the agent should classify the issue (refund, order status, product question), retrieve the customer's order history, and draft a response. The store owner should be able to review and approve the response before it's sent."

Step 2 of 6 — Vision Capture5 min

Answer five questions — intentionally unstructured

QuestionPurpose
What problem does this application solve?Define the mission
Who are the users?Define the audience
What is the primary workflow?Define the flow
What does success look like?Define the outcome
What are the constraints?Define the boundaries
Applied to our example

Problem: support tickets take too long to triage and answer manually.

Users: the store owner (approver) and customers (ticket submitters).

Workflow: ticket in → classify → pull order history → draft reply → owner approves → send.

Success: faster first-response time, without the owner losing final say.

Constraints: nothing sends without human approval; must read the existing order database.

Step 3 of 6 — Blueprint Generation6 min

Hand Claude your raw vision with a structured architect prompt

Ask it to act as architect, and to ask questions before generating anything:

Here is my vision for an application. I need you to act as my architect. Please help me structure this into a complete blueprint. Ask me clarifying questions about anything that's ambiguous. Then produce a comprehensive plan that includes: The application's purpose and scope The user workflow (step by step) The system architecture (components and their relationships) The file system structure The technology stack (platform, language, frameworks) The data model The key risks and constraints The phased implementation roadmap Ask me questions before generating the plan to make sure you understand the full context.

Claude then engages in a real dialogue — surfacing gaps you wouldn't have thought to name yourself, not just filling a template.

Excerpt from our example's blueprint

Expected Behavior: Agent classifies into refund / order-status / product-question. Draft is always held for owner approval — no auto-send path exists, even for "obvious" cases.

Open Question Claude raised: "What happens if the agent can't classify the ticket confidently — hold it for manual triage, or guess and flag it?"

Step 4 of 6 — Refinement5 min

The single most important refinement question in this whole phase

"What are the open questions?"

You repeat this small loop as many rounds as you want. A quick out exists if you don't want to plan much — generate the plan after one round. If you want to plan extensively, the loop keeps track of everything while you keep exploring. This keeps you in control two ways: you make the decisions, and you decide how much to plan.

Other refinement prompts — click each

"What are the open questions?"▸
Surface what remains ambiguous — the default first question every round.
"What are the key risks I haven't considered?"▸
Identify blind spots — for our example, this is what surfaced the low-confidence classification edge case.
"Can you provide a diagram of the data flow?"▸
Visualize the architecture — useful once the components list starts feeling abstract.
"What are the trade-offs in this architecture?"▸
Make informed decisions — e.g. real-time classification vs. batch processing.
"What would a minimal viable version look like?"▸
Scope the first release — for our example: skip order-history lookup in v1, just classify and draft.
Resolving our example's open question

Answer: "Hold for manual triage — never guess on a classification that would change what data gets pulled or who approves it."

Step 5 of 6 — Final Plan & Tools6 min

The final blueprint becomes a contract

The completed blueprint is the "source of truth" for the entire project — the input Phase 2 executes against, and the contract between you (the architect) and the AI (the builder).

PhaseHow the blueprint is used
Phase 2Handed to Claude Code as the specification for building the application
Phase 3Informs the agent architecture decisions
TestingDefines expected behavior — tests are derived from it
DocumentationBecomes the project's foundational documentation

The same process, automated — the "Blueprint" skill

Everything you just did by hand in Steps 2–4 is what the imbue-ai/blueprint skill automates, compatible with Claude Code, Codex CLI, Gemini CLI, and other agent harnesses:

1. Run /blueprint <description> — your raw vision from Step 1
2. Pick a template — Default or Concise
3. It explores your codebase and asks the Step 2/3 clarifying questions for you
4. You answer what matters, skip what you don't care about
5. Run /blueprint-generate — the same 9-section plan is written to blueprint/<slug>/plan-<slug>.md
"Blueprint splits planning between you and the agent. The initial idea comes from you. Enumerating all the considerations and choices comes from the agent. Decisions come from you. You skip the tedious parts of planning but stay in the driver's seat."

A deeper variant — the "Agent Architect" questionnaire

For agent-specific projects, a structured interview generates seven production-grade workspace files: SOUL.md, IDENTITY.md, AGENTS.md, OPERATIONS.md, TOOLS.md, MEMORY.md, HEARTBEAT.md.
