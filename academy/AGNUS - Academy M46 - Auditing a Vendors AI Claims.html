AGNUS Academy Level 7 · Govern M46 — Auditing a Vendor's AI Claims
Step 1 of 4 — Understand4 min

A vendor's marketing claim about their own AI deserves the same scrutiny as your own team's output.

Every verification instinct built across this Academy — checking a specific figure, tracing a claim to its source — applies just as much to a third party's sales pitch as it does to your own agent's draft.

Why this matters

It's easy to apply verification-first scrutiny to your own team's work while accepting a vendor's confident claims about their own product at face value — "99.9% accuracy" or "enterprise-grade security" sound authoritative precisely because they're meant to. A vendor's unverified claim carries the same real risk to your organization as an internal one, once you're relying on it.

Step 2 of 4 — See4 min

A vendor claim, and the questions a real audit asks

From vendor's sales deck

"Our AI model achieves 99.9% accuracy in detecting fraudulent transactions, validated across our enterprise client base."

What a real audit asks, that a sales deck doesn't answer

1Accuracy measured how — against what specific dataset, and how large and representative was it?
2Does "99.9% accuracy" account for the actual rate of fraud in real transactions (a rare event), where even a high accuracy figure can still mean a large number of false positives or missed fraud in absolute terms?
3Is this figure independently verifiable, or is it a number the vendor generated internally with no external audit?
4Would the vendor provide the underlying test methodology on request, or does "validated" mean something looser than a real, reproducible test?

None of this means the vendor is lying. It means "99.9% accuracy" is a claim, not evidence — and a governance-minded buyer asks for the evidence before making a purchasing or deployment decision based on it.

Step 3 of 4 — Practice4 min

A vendor claims their AI hiring tool is "bias-free." What is the appropriate governance response before adopting it?

AAccept the claim, since the vendor presumably tested it themselves.
BRequest the specific methodology and results behind that claim, and consider running your own independent bias evaluation before deploying it on real hiring decisions.
CRefuse to work with any AI vendor at all, regardless of their claims.
DAssume "bias-free" is a legally binding guarantee with no need for further checking.
EAsk a different vendor if their tool is more or less bias-free by comparison, without checking either claim.

A vendor's compliance documentation states their AI system is "fully compliant with all applicable regulations." What is the strongest reason to treat this specific phrasing with caution?

A"All applicable regulations" is vague about which regulations, in which jurisdictions, were actually assessed — a real compliance claim should name the specific frameworks checked, not gesture at completeness in the abstract.
BNo caution is needed — vendors are legally required to only state true things in documentation.
CThis phrasing is fine as long as the document is long.
DCompliance claims never need any scrutiny once written down formally.
EThis should be trusted more than a specific claim, since it covers more ground.
