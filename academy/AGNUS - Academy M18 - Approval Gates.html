An approval gate is a
deliberate pause for human judgment.

Approval gates are the mechanism that keeps humans in the loop for consequential decisions. They are not inefficiency — they are designed checkpoints where the cost of being wrong is high enough that autonomous agent action is not appropriate.

6 steps ~15 min Includes interactive gate simulation

What an approval gate is

An approval gate is a point in an agent's workflow where it stops and waits for human confirmation before proceeding. The agent has prepared an action — assembled the data, verified the conditions, generated the output — but has not executed it. The gate gives a human the opportunity to review, modify, or reject the proposed action before it takes effect.

Approval gates are not checks on the agent's intelligence — they are designed control points based on the operator's assessment of where autonomous action carries unacceptable risk. A well-designed approval gate captures the efficiency gains of agent automation while maintaining human authority over the decisions that matter most.

Step 2 of 6Anatomy of an approval gate
4 min
Approval gate flow — invoice payment example
Prepare
Agent reads invoice, matches PO, calculates amount. Prepares payment instruction but does not submit.
Gate — Human reviews
Agent presents: payee, amount, PO reference, invoice date. Human reviews. Approves or rejects.
Approved path
Human approves. Agent submits payment and writes audit log.
Rejected path
Human rejects. Agent logs the rejection with reason. No payment submitted.

Three components of every approval gate

The trigger condition: The specific criteria that cause the agent to pause and request approval — amount threshold, action type, data sensitivity, external communication.

The presentation package: What information the agent gives the human at the gate. A gate that presents insufficient information for the human to make a good decision is a gate that will be approved by default — not by judgment.

The action paths: What happens if approved, what happens if rejected, what happens if there is no response within a timeout. All three must be defined.

Step 3 of 6Trigger conditions for approval gates

Trigger typeExample conditionWhy this trigger matters
ThresholdPayment amount exceeds €500Financial risk increases with amount. A threshold-based gate scales oversight proportionally to consequence.
Action typeAny external communication (email to customers or suppliers)External communications represent the company publicly. Errors are visible and may create legal commitments.
Novel conditionInvoice has no matching PO, or first transaction with a new supplierNew or unexpected conditions have higher error rates. A gate for novel conditions catches outliers.
Data sensitivityAction involves personal data, medical data, or financial data of a third partyHigh-sensitivity data processing requires human accountability — both ethically and under GDPR.
IrreversibilityAction cannot be undone — submit payment, delete record, send emailReversible actions can be corrected. Irreversible ones cannot. Gates on irreversible actions are the most critical.
Not every action needs a gate. A gate on every single agent action defeats the purpose of automation and trains approvers to click through without reading. Gates should be placed where the cost of being wrong is high — not where they create the appearance of oversight without the substance.

Step 5 of 6Practice
3 min

A team designs an approval gate for customer service agents that sends an alert for every single response before it is sent to the customer. After two weeks, the team notices that approvers are clicking "approve" on every single alert within 3–5 seconds without reading them. Customer service response time has doubled.

What went wrong with this gate design, and what is the principle it violated?
A
The gate was too slow — it needs a faster approval interface.
B
The gate was placed on every action regardless of risk. When approvers are presented with too many low-risk gates, they treat all gates as routine and click through without reading — including the ones that actually matter. Gates should be reserved for actions where the cost of being wrong is genuinely high. A gate on every response trained approvers to ignore gates entirely.
C
Approvers need better training on how to review gate requests.
Final test
Step 6 of 6Pass — hidden test
3 min

An agent's approval gate is triggered when a payment exceeds €5,000. The gate presents to the approver: "Payment ready: €6,200. Approve or reject?" The approver consistently approves within seconds. A review reveals that approvers don't have enough information to make a meaningful decision — they don't know the payee, the invoice reference, or the PO match status from the gate presentation.

Which component of the approval gate is broken?
A
The trigger condition — €5,000 is too low a threshold.
B
The presentation package. The gate is triggering correctly (€6,200 exceeds €5,000 threshold) but presenting insufficient information for the approver to make a real decision. Without payee, invoice reference, and PO match status, "approve or reject" becomes a coin flip. A gate with a poor presentation package trains approvers to approve by default rather than by judgment.
C
The action paths — the reject path isn't defined clearly enough.
