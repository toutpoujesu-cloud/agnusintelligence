AGNUS Academy Level 5 · Verify M34 — Verifying a Data Analysis Conclusion
Module 34 of 49 · Level 5 · Verify

The data was right.
The conclusion was wrong.

An AI can correctly extract, calculate, and present data — and still draw the wrong conclusion from it. Data verification and conclusion verification are different disciplines. This module focuses on the second: how to assess whether the conclusion an AI draws from data is actually supported by that data.

6 steps ~15 min Prerequisite: M33

Two separate verification problems

M30 covered data verification: are the numbers correct? This module covers conclusion verification: given those numbers, does the stated conclusion actually follow?

An agent can present perfectly correct data and draw a logically invalid conclusion from it. The data passes M30 verification. But the analysis fails M34 verification. Both matter — and they require different checks.

Next: the five logical errors
Step 2 of 6Five logical errors in AI data analysis
4 min
1
Correlation presented as causation
Two variables move together in the data. The AI concludes one caused the other, without evidence of the causal mechanism.
"Customer satisfaction scores increased in the same quarter we launched the new UI. The new UI drove the improvement." — Correct correlation; unproven causation. Other factors changed in the same quarter.
2
Trend extrapolation without basis
A short-term trend is projected forward as if it will continue indefinitely, without acknowledging the uncertainty or the factors that could change it.
"Revenue has grown 25% for 3 consecutive quarters. At this rate, the company will double within 18 months." — 3 quarters is not a reliable basis for 18-month projections in most markets.
3
Average masking distribution
An average is presented as representative when the distribution is highly skewed or bimodal. The average is correct, but it doesn't describe what most of the data actually looks like.
"Average order value increased from €85 to €120." — If 5% of orders now account for 60% of revenue, the median may have fallen. The average is technically correct but misleading.
4
Selection bias in the sample
The data was collected from a non-representative subset, and the conclusion is applied to a broader population as if the subset were representative.
"Based on survey responses, 78% of users prefer Feature X." — If only power users completed the survey, this doesn't represent the broader user base.
5
Absence of evidence as evidence of absence
No data was found showing a negative effect, so the AI concludes no negative effect exists. This confuses "we didn't measure it" with "it doesn't happen."
"No customer complaints were received about the new feature. The rollout was successful." — If the new feature suppressed the feedback mechanism, no complaints would appear even if users were dissatisfied.
Next: the five verification questions
Step 3 of 6The five conclusion verification questions
4 min
Q1
Does the conclusion match the data's scope?
A conclusion drawn from data about one market should not be applied to another market. A conclusion from one time period should not be projected forward without qualification.
Q2
Is causation claimed where only correlation is shown?
Look for causal language: "drove," "caused," "led to," "because of." If the data only shows that two things moved together, causal language is not supported.
Q3
Is the average representative, or would median/distribution tell a different story?
When averages are used, ask: is the distribution normal, or is there significant skew? A mean can be very different from what most observations look like.
Q4
Could the sample be biased?
Who responded? Who was included? Are there groups whose data was not captured? Survivorship bias, voluntary response bias, and selection bias can all produce correct numbers from an unrepresentative sample.
Q5
What alternative conclusions could the same data support?
This is the most important question. An AI will produce one conclusion. Ask it: "What other conclusions could this data support?" If the data is compatible with multiple conclusions, the stated conclusion is not the only one — and the most convenient conclusion is often not the most honest one.
Next: a worked walkthrough
Step 4 of 6A worked walkthrough
4 min
Conclusion verification — AI churn analysis
Element
Agent output and verification
Agent conclusion
"The new onboarding flow caused a 22% reduction in churn. The redesign should be rolled out to all user segments."
Data presented
Churn rate fell from 8.1% to 6.3% in the 3 months following the new onboarding launch. N=1,840 new users in the test group.
Q1 — Scope
Test group was new users in the enterprise segment. "All user segments" includes SMB and individual plans — not represented in the test group.
Q2 — Causation
ISSUE: "caused" is causal language. The test coincided with a major product update and a marketing campaign targeting the same segment. The correlation is present; causation is not established.
Q3 — Average
Churn rate is a population metric — less affected by distribution skew. No issue here.
Q4 — Sample bias
ISSUE: New users who churned before completing onboarding were not in the measurement window — they may have churned before the new flow was experienced. This survivorship bias understates early churn.
Q5 — Alternatives
ISSUE: Alternative conclusion supported by the same data: "Churn improved in the enterprise segment during Q3 — possibly due to the product update, the marketing campaign, or the onboarding redesign, or a combination. The relative contribution of each factor is not isolated."
Verdict
The data is correct. The conclusion overstates causation, has scope issues, survivorship bias, and does not present alternative explanations. Not ready for a "roll out to all segments" recommendation.

Step 5 of 6Practice
3 min

An AI agent analyses customer support data and concludes: "Customers who use the live chat feature have a 94% satisfaction score, compared to 71% for customers who use email support. We should push customers to use live chat to improve overall satisfaction." The data is correct.

Which logical error is most prominent in this conclusion?
A
Trend extrapolation — the agent is projecting a short-term pattern forward.
B
Correlation presented as causation, combined with selection bias. Live chat users may self-select — they are more technically comfortable, have simpler questions, or are already more satisfied customers. The high satisfaction score may describe a different type of customer, not the effect of the channel. Pushing all customers to live chat won't replicate the channel effect if the effect is actually a customer type effect.
C
Average masking distribution — the satisfaction scores may be skewed.
Final test
Step 6 of 6Pass — hidden test
3 min

An AI analysis shows: "In the regions where we ran the AI-assisted sales pilot, revenue grew 18% compared to 6% in control regions. The pilot drove 12 percentage points of outperformance. We should roll out the pilot nationally." A team member applies Q5 and asks the AI: "What alternative conclusions could this data support?" The AI responds: "The pilot regions were selected because they had higher growth potential. The difference may reflect the selection criteria rather than the pilot's effect."

What should happen next, and what does this illustrate about Q5?
A
The national rollout should be cancelled — the alternative conclusion means the pilot didn't work.
B
The analysis needs to control for the selection criteria before a rollout recommendation can be made. The AI's alternative conclusion (selection effect vs. pilot effect) is a testable hypothesis — if regions were selected for growth potential, the analysis should compare pilot regions against similar-potential regions, not all non-pilot regions. This illustrates that Q5 doesn't invalidate the conclusion — it identifies what needs to be verified before the conclusion can be accepted.
C
The AI produced the alternative conclusion itself, which means it already considered it and still recommended the rollout.
