Marketing claims are the
most legally consequential AI output.

A wrong financial number in an internal report is a problem. A wrong claim in marketing material — on a website, in a pitch, in advertising — may be a consumer law violation, a misleading commercial claim, or the basis for a regulatory complaint. This module applies the M30 verification standard to marketing-specific claim types.

6 steps ~15 min Prerequisite: M30

Why marketing claims are different from internal claims

When an AI agent produces incorrect data for an internal report, the cost is correction effort and potential decision errors. When an AI agent produces incorrect claims for external marketing — "the most trusted platform in Europe," "reduces costs by 60%," "used by 10,000 organisations" — the consequences are different: consumer law violations, competitor complaints, regulatory investigation, and loss of customer trust.

Consumer protection legislation in the UK, EU, and Norway requires that commercial claims be accurate, substantiated, and not misleading. An AI-generated marketing claim that cannot be substantiated with real data is a regulatory risk — regardless of whether the operator intended to mislead.

Next: the five marketing claim categories
Step 2 of 6Five marketing claim categories
4 min
Category 1
Comparative claims
"Faster than X." "The most Y." "Better than the competition." These require direct, fair, current comparisons against named or identifiable competitors. AI agents often invent or extrapolate comparative claims.
Verify: Is the comparison current? Is it fair (same measurement)? Is it from an independent source?
Category 2
Quantified performance claims
"Reduces costs by 40%." "Saves 5 hours per week." These must be based on real data from real customers in similar contexts. Invented or extrapolated figures are unlawful.
Verify: Source for the figure? Sample size? Methodology? Conditions under which the result was achieved?
Category 3
Social proof claims
"Used by 10,000 organisations." "Trusted by Fortune 500 companies." These require a real count, real organisations, and real use — not logos displayed on a website without permission or users who signed up but never actively used the product.
Verify: Real customer count? Active users? Named permission for logo use?
Category 4
Award and certification claims
"Award-winning." "Industry-recognised." "Certified." These require real awards or certifications from named bodies. AI agents routinely fabricate plausible-sounding award names.
Verify: Named award, named issuer, date received. Award must still be current and valid.
Category 5
Superlative claims
"The leading platform." "Europe's most trusted." "The only solution that…" Superlatives require market evidence — ideally independent research. "The only solution that X" requires demonstrable knowledge that no competitor does X.
Verify: Evidence for the superlative? Independent source? Current? Can a competitor challenge this?
The test
Can you substantiate it?
If a regulator, competitor, or journalist asked "how do you know this?" — do you have a real, documented, current answer? If not, the claim is unsubstantiated regardless of how accurate it sounds.

Step 3 of 6The incident — traced step by step
4 min
Case study
How an AI-generated product page created a consumer law exposure
1
The brief
"Write a product page for our AI compliance platform. Highlight our competitive advantages and use compelling statistics."
2
The agent's output — selected claims
"Trusted by over 2,000 organisations across Europe." "Reduces compliance workload by up to 65%." "Named the leading AI governance platform by industry analysts." "The only platform to combine real-time monitoring with automated regulatory updates."
3
What was reviewed
The marketing team reviewed the page for tone, clarity, and brand consistency. They did not verify the specific claims.
No substantiation check was performed. The page went live with all four claims.
4
What a competitor found
A competitor filed a complaint with the advertising standards body. "2,000 organisations" — the actual count was ~340 active paying customers. "65% reduction" — no documented evidence. "Named by analysts" — no such report exists. "Only platform" — competitor has the same feature.
All four claims were challenged. None could be substantiated at the level required by consumer law.
5
The fix
Page taken down. Claims rewritten to reflect substantiated figures. Verification policy added to all AI-generated marketing output.
Prevention: every marketing claim category requires substantiation evidence before publication. "Compelling statistics" in a brief must be replaced with "accurate, substantiated statistics."
Next: the substantiation standard
Step 4 of 6The substantiation standard
3 min

Every external claim needs a substantiation file

Before any AI-generated marketing claim goes live, it needs a substantiation file — the documented evidence that supports each specific claim. This is not bureaucracy; it's the minimum required to defend the claim if challenged.

Substantiation file — per claim

CLAIM: "Reduces compliance workload by up to 40%"
TYPE: Quantified performance claim
SOURCE: Customer survey, Q2 2026, n=48 active enterprise customers
METHODOLOGY: Self-reported time saved on compliance documentation tasks, before/after comparison
EXACT FIGURE: Median: 38%, Maximum: 61%, "Up to 40%" is within the observed range
QUALIFICATIONS: "Results may vary. Based on enterprise customers in regulated industries."
DATE: Survey conducted May 2026. Must be refreshed by May 2027.
REVIEWER: Legal / Marketing Director sign-off required before publication.
The brief must change. "Use compelling statistics" is an instruction that will produce invented statistics. Replace it with: "Use only statistics from our approved claim library. If no statistic exists for a claim type, flag it for substantiation before use — do not invent one."
Next: practice
Step 5 of 6Practice
3 min

An AI agent produces a press release for a product launch. One sentence reads: "Our platform is already trusted by leading organisations in healthcare, finance, and legal sectors across 12 European countries." The marketing director says: "That's roughly accurate — we do have customers in those sectors." A verification lead says: "We need to substantiate each element before this goes out."

What specific elements require substantiation?
A
Only the "12 European countries" figure — that's the specific numerical claim.
B
All three elements require substantiation: (1) "already trusted" — what does "trusted" mean here? Is there evidence beyond having paying customers? (2) "leading organisations" — are the healthcare, finance, and legal customers actually "leading" organisations in their sectors, or is this an inflated description? (3) "12 European countries" — is the actual customer country count 12? This is a factual claim that must match the CRM data exactly.
C
Nothing requires substantiation — the director confirmed it's "roughly accurate."
Final test
Step 6 of 6Pass — hidden test
3 min

A product manager argues: "If we require full substantiation for every AI-generated marketing claim, we lose the speed advantage of AI content generation." A legal counsel responds: "The speed advantage of AI is in drafting — not in bypassing the substantiation requirement that would apply to any claim, AI-generated or not."

Who is right, and what is the key insight?
A
The product manager is right — substantiation requirements were designed for slower content creation processes and should be adapted for AI speed.
B
The legal counsel is right. Consumer law doesn't have a "this was AI-generated" exception. The substantiation requirement applies to the claim — not to who or what generated it. AI accelerates the drafting of marketing content. It doesn't change what's required to publish it legally. The speed gain is in drafting and iteration, not in bypassing the substantiation step that would always have been required.
C
Both are right — a proportionate substantiation approach should apply lower standards to AI-generated content.
