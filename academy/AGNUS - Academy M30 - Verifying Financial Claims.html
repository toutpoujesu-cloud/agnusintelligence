Why financial claims are the hardest to verify

A financial claim made by an AI agent — a revenue figure, a cost percentage, a market size, a growth rate — looks authoritative. It's specific, it's numerical, and it appears in a well-formatted output. But language models can produce plausible-sounding financial figures that are wrong, outdated, or extrapolated from incomplete training data.

Numerical specificity is not evidence of numerical accuracy. A claim that "the company's gross margin was 43.2% in Q3" is either directly verifiable from the financial statements or it's an invention — and the number's precision is not a clue which.

Next: the four types of financial claim
Step 2 of 6Four types of financial claim — and how to verify each
4 min
Type 1
Reported financials
Revenue, profit, costs, margins from a specific period. These exist in primary documents: annual reports, audited accounts, official filings.
Verify: find the source document. Check the exact figure. Note the reporting period.
Type 2
Market data and statistics
Market size, growth rates, industry percentages. These come from research firms (IDC, Gartner, Statista) and are frequently misquoted, outdated, or paraphrased incorrectly.
Verify: find the specific report, the publication date, the exact figure, and the methodology. "Source: Gartner" without a report name is not a citation.
Type 3
Calculated figures
Ratios, percentages, derived values. These may be calculated correctly from correct inputs — or may use wrong inputs or wrong formulas.
Verify: re-calculate from primary data. Do not verify a ratio by checking whether it looks reasonable.
Type 4
Projections and forecasts
Future figures, growth estimates, ROI calculations. These are inherently uncertain — but agents often present them with the same confidence as historical facts.
Verify: identify the assumptions. A projection is only as reliable as its inputs and methodology. Ask: what does this assume, and is that assumption stated?

Step 3 of 6The five-step financial claim verification standard
4 min
StepWhat to checkPass condition
1. Source existsIs there a real, identifiable primary source for this claim? Not "it's plausible" — a named document, report, or dataset.Named primary source with URL or publication reference.
2. Source is currentIs the source recent enough for the context? A 2019 market size figure cited in a 2026 strategy document is not current.Source date is appropriate for the claim's use. If outdated, note the gap explicitly.
3. Figure is exactDoes the number in the agent's output exactly match the number in the source? Not "roughly consistent" — exact match or explicitly qualified.Exact match, or stated as approximation with reference to the exact source figure.
4. Context is preservedIs the figure being used in the same context as the source intended? "Revenue grew 40%" may be correct for one product line but not for the company overall.Scope, segment, geography, and time period all match between source and use.
5. Calculation is correctFor derived figures: re-calculate from primary data. Does the formula produce the stated result from the stated inputs?Independent re-calculation confirms the derived figure.

Step 4 of 6A worked verification example

Verification walkthrough — AI-generated financial summary
"Nordvik Group reported revenue of €14.2M in FY2025, a 22% increase from FY2024. Gross margin improved to 38.4%, up from 34.1% the prior year."
1
Source exists? → Find the FY2025 annual report or audited accounts for Nordvik Group.
Found: Annual Report 2025, published 2026-03-15. Verified source exists.
2
Source current? → FY2025 report published March 2026, used in an August 2026 strategy brief.
Acceptable — most recent available year.
3
Figure exact? → Annual Report p.4: "Revenue FY2025: €14.2M. FY2024: €11.6M." Agent stated €14.2M.
Revenue: exact match ✓.
3b
22% growth claim → €11.6M to €14.2M = 22.4% growth. Agent stated 22%.
Minor rounding — acceptable if noted as approximate.
3c
Gross margin 38.4% → Annual Report p.7: Gross profit €5.1M / Revenue €14.2M = 35.9%. Agent stated 38.4%.
MISMATCH — 38.4% ≠ 35.9%. Either wrong figure or wrong year's data. Do not use without correction.
4
Context preserved? → Revenue figures apply to the full group, same as the source uses.
Context matches ✓.
The margin figure failed Step 3. Revenue was correct and the growth rate was close. But the gross margin diverged by 2.5 percentage points — enough to change the narrative about the business. Step 3 (exact match) requires re-calculation from the source, not reading comprehension of the summary.

Step 5 of 6Practice

An agent produces a competitive analysis: "The AI compliance software market is valued at $4.2B and is projected to grow at 34% CAGR through 2030, according to a recent industry report." A team member says: "That matches what I've been seeing — sounds right." A verification lead says: "We need the specific report, the publisher, the date, and the exact page before this can be used in a board presentation."

Which verification step is at issue?
A
Step 1 — source exists. "A recent industry report" is not a named primary source. Without the publisher, report title, and date, the claim cannot be verified against a real document. The claim may be correct — but it is unverified.
B
Step 4 — context preservation. The scope of the market definition may not match Nordvik's specific use case.
C
Step 2 — source currency. The report may be outdated.
Final test
Step 6 of 6Pass — hidden test
3 min

A finance analyst uses an AI agent to produce a board report section. The agent states: "Gross profit margin improved from 31% to 37% year-on-year, representing a 6 percentage point improvement." The analyst verifies the source annual report and confirms: gross profit in year 1 was €3.1M on revenue of €10M (31%), and gross profit in year 2 was €4.44M on revenue of €12M (37%). The analyst marks this section as verified.

Is the verification complete?
A
Yes — the source figures match the percentages stated by the agent.
B
Not quite. The percentages are correct. But Step 4 (context preservation) requires checking that the "6 percentage point improvement" framing is not misleading. A 6 pp improvement from 31% to 37% is mathematically correct — but the analyst should confirm whether this is presented in a context where the absolute improvement in gross profit (€1.34M on €2M more revenue) would be more informative. The verification is technically correct but the analyst should flag the framing question to the author.
C
No — the gross profit figures need to be independently recalculated from the raw financial data, not just confirmed in the annual report.
