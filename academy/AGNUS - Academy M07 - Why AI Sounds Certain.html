The mechanism behind the certainty

In M05 you learned that confidence is a stylistic property of AI output, not an accuracy signal. In M07 you'll understand the structural reason why — what is actually happening inside the model when it produces a confident but wrong statement, and why it cannot behave differently without being explicitly engineered to do so.

This isn't about trust. It's about understanding the architecture so you can design your workflows around it correctly.

Next: how the model generates text
Step 2 of 6How a model generates text
4 min

Prediction, not retrieval

A language model does not look up facts. It predicts the most likely next word (token) given everything that came before it. It does this one token at a time, using statistical patterns learned from its training data.

When you ask "What is the capital of Norway?", the model doesn't search a database and return "Oslo." It predicts that "Oslo" is the most likely token to follow in a sequence that starts with a question about Norway's capital — because in its training data, that sequence appeared overwhelmingly in contexts where "Oslo" followed.

This distinction matters enormously. Retrieval produces the stored value or reports that it doesn't exist. Prediction produces the most likely token — whether or not the underlying fact exists.

The structural consequence

The model has no "I don't know" switch

A database returns null when it has no record. A human expert says "I don't know" when they haven't encountered the fact. A language model has no equivalent. When the most likely next token is a confident factual statement, it produces a confident factual statement — regardless of whether the fact is real. The absence of a stored fact doesn't produce silence. It produces the next most likely token, which is often a plausible-sounding invented fact.

Next: three mechanisms that produce wrong-but-confident output
Step 3 of 6Three mechanisms that produce wrong-but-confident output
4 min
Mechanism 1
Pattern completion
The model has seen many court cases cited in legal writing. When it generates a legal analysis, it completes the pattern: plausible case name, plausible year, plausible holding. The pattern is correct even when the specific instance is fabricated.
Mechanism 2
Training data proximity
The model's knowledge is distributed across billions of training examples. For some topics it has dense, accurate coverage. For others — obscure regulations, recent events, your organisation's specific policies — the coverage is sparse or absent. But the output tone is the same in both cases.
Mechanism 3
Confident prose is more likely
In most training data, confident declarative sentences are more common than hedged ones. "The regulation requires X" appears more often than "The regulation may require X, but this is uncertain." So the model predicts confident prose by default — not because it is certain, but because confident prose has higher statistical frequency.

What this means for you as an operator

These three mechanisms operate at all times, on all topics, in all AI systems based on language models. They are not bugs to be fixed — they are properties of the architecture. A more advanced model does not eliminate them; it reduces their frequency on well-covered topics while maintaining the same confident output style on everything.

Your governance response to this is not to distrust AI entirely — it's to know which outputs require verification and to build verification steps into your workflows at those points.

Step 4 of 6What to do differently
3 min

Two types of AI output — two different approaches

Once you understand the mechanism, you can classify AI outputs into two categories and handle them differently.

High-stakes specific claims — verify
Legal citations, regulatory requirements, statistics, named company data, pricing, dates, case law, specific technical specifications. These are the outputs most vulnerable to hallucination — specific details are exactly what Pattern Completion generates most convincingly. Every one of these must be verified against a primary source before use.
Structural and reasoning outputs — review, don't verify
Organising information into a structure, drafting prose from facts you provided, generating options for a decision, summarising a document you supplied. Here the model is applying pattern and reasoning to inputs you control — less susceptible to hallucination. Human review for quality is appropriate; primary-source verification of every sentence is not.
The AGNUS principle: Every FACT statement in AGNUS Research carries a source citation — not because the AI is unreliable as a thinking tool, but because specific factual claims require primary-source verification regardless of how confident they sound. The AI drafts. The source verifies.

A team is using an AI assistant for two tasks: (A) "List five potential risks of deploying an AI agent in our customer service workflow" and (B) "What is the exact fine threshold for violations of Article 5 of the EU AI Act?" The team decides they don't need to verify either output because "the AI was very clear and specific in both cases."

Which task's output requires primary-source verification, and why?
A
Both require verification — AI output is always unreliable regardless of content type.
B
Task B requires primary-source verification. The fine threshold is a specific legal fact — exactly the type of detail that Pattern Completion fabricates convincingly. Task A (listing potential risks) is a reasoning and structuring task applied to a general topic — the model is less likely to hallucinate "risk categories" than specific statutory figures. Task B must be verified against the actual EU AI Act text.
C
Task A requires verification — risks are subjective and the AI might have a bias.

Step 6 of 6Pass — hidden test
3 min

A manager tells their team: "We should use a newer, more advanced AI model — it'll be less likely to make things up." Another team member responds: "A better model helps, but it doesn't solve the underlying issue."

Who is right, and what is the "underlying issue" the second team member is referring to?
A
The manager is right — newer models are trained on more data and are significantly more accurate, effectively solving the hallucination problem.
B
The second team member is right. A better model reduces hallucination frequency on well-covered topics, but does not change the structural property: the model predicts the most likely token regardless of whether the underlying fact exists. The underlying issue is that no language model has an "I don't know" switch. Verification workflows are needed regardless of model quality — they become slightly less burdensome with better models, but they don't disappear.
C
Both are wrong — the real issue is that AI shouldn't be used for factual work at all.
