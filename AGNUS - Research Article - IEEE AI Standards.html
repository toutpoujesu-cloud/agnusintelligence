IEEE Standards for AI: What Engineers and Deployers Need to Know

ISO and NIST dominate AI governance discussions, but IEEE has developed a body of technical AI standards that are directly relevant to practitioners — particularly those building or deploying AI systems where technical specificity matters. This article maps the most relevant IEEE AI standards, explains their relationship to ISO and NIST frameworks, and identifies where IEEE fills gaps that governance frameworks leave open.

Jean-Daniel Alexis· 11 September 2026· Standards pillar· AR-000720

IEEE's role in AI standardisation

The Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) develops technical standards across engineering domains. In AI, IEEE has focused on three areas: transparency and interpretability of automated decisions (IEEE 7001, IEEE 7010), the ethics of autonomous systems (IEEE 7000 series broadly), and algorithmic bias (IEEE 2894).

IEEE standards differ from ISO management system standards (like ISO 42001) in their orientation: they tend to be more technical and more specific about how systems should behave, rather than how organisations should be governed. They provide engineering guidance where governance frameworks provide organisational guidance.

For AI governance practitioners, IEEE standards are most relevant when: designing systems that must demonstrate accountability for automated decisions; evaluating whether an AI system meets transparency requirements; or specifying technical requirements for AI procurement. They complement, rather than replace, ISO and NIST frameworks.

The key IEEE AI standards

StandardTitle and scopeKey requirementsStatus
IEEE 7001-2021 Transparency of Autonomous Systems. Defines five levels of transparency and the conditions under which each level is appropriate. Systems must document which decisions are automated, what information is used, and what the system can and cannot explain about its decisions. Five transparency levels from "no explanation" to "full causal explanation." Published 2021
IEEE 7010-2020 Recommended Practice for Assessing the Impact of Autonomous and Intelligent Systems on Human Well-Being. Provides a methodology for wellbeing impact assessment. Defines metrics for eight wellbeing dimensions (education, work, relationships, safety, etc.) and a methodology for assessing AI system impact on each. Precursor to the EU AI Act's FRIA requirement. Published 2020
IEEE 2894-2024 Guide for an Ethical Framework for Artificial Intelligence and Automated Decision Systems. Covers fairness definitions, bias measurement, and accountability structures. Provides guidance on fairness metric selection, bias testing methodology, accountability chain documentation, and the relationship between technical bias metrics and legal discrimination standards. Published 2024
IEEE 7000-2021 Model Process for Addressing Ethical Concerns During System Design. Provides a process for embedding ethical considerations into the system engineering lifecycle. Identifies "value-based engineering" as a design practice. Requires consideration of stakeholder values at each phase of system design. Complementary to ISO 42001. Published 2021
IEEE P3119 Standard for the Procurement of Artificial Intelligence and Automated Decision Systems. Covers what buyers should require from AI vendors. Specifies vendor documentation requirements, performance testing standards, and contractual provisions for AI procurement. Directly relevant to deployer governance. In development
IEEE P2976 Recommended Practice for Privacy and Security for Federated Machine Learning. Technical guidance for privacy-preserving ML. Covers federated learning security, model inversion defences, differential privacy implementation, and secure aggregation protocols. In development

IEEE vs. ISO 42001 vs. NIST AI RMF

IEEE: Technical and engineering-oriented. Tells you how systems should behave and how to measure behaviour. Most useful for developers, AI architects, and technical auditors. Does not prescribe organisational management systems.

ISO 42001: Management system standard. Tells you how to organise and manage AI governance. Most useful for organisations seeking certification, demonstrating compliance, and structuring governance programmes. Does not specify technical system behaviour in detail.

NIST AI RMF: Risk management framework. Tells you how to organise risk management work. Most useful as a process framework for implementing AI risk management. Does not prescribe specific metrics or technical tests.

The three frameworks are complementary. A complete AI governance programme might use ISO 42001 as the management system structure, NIST AI RMF as the risk management process, and IEEE standards as the technical specifications for how specific systems should behave and be tested.

For practitioners: where IEEE adds most value

Transparency requirements. The EU AI Act requires transparency for high-risk AI (Article 13) and for general-purpose AI (Article 50). It specifies that systems must provide enough information for users to make informed decisions, but does not specify what technical transparency mechanisms are required. IEEE 7001's five transparency levels provide a practical implementation framework.

Fairness metric selection. The EU AI Act requires that high-risk AI systems achieve an appropriate level of accuracy across different groups (Article 15). It does not specify which fairness metric is appropriate. IEEE 2894 provides guidance on fairness metric selection and the trade-offs between metrics — filling a technical gap that the EU AI Act leaves open.

Procurement specifications. When procuring AI from vendors, IEEE P3119 (once finalised) will provide a technical specification for what buyers should require — complementing the governance framework approach in AGNUS's AI Procurement Governance article.
