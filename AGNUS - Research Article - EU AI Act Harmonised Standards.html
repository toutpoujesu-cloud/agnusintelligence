Harmonised Standards Under the EU AI Act: What They Are and Why They Matter

The EU AI Act relies on harmonised standards to translate its requirements into specific technical specifications. When a harmonised standard is cited in the Official Journal of the EU, compliance with that standard creates a legal presumption of conformity with the corresponding EU AI Act requirements. As of September 2026, no harmonised AI standards have been cited — but the standardisation process is underway. This article explains the mechanism and its implications.

Jean-Daniel Alexis· 11 September 2026· Standards pillar· AR-000730

How harmonised standards work under EU law

The New Approach to technical regulation, established by EU product legislation since 1985, separates regulatory requirements from technical specifications. The law states what outcomes are required (safety, performance, reliability); technical standards specify how to achieve those outcomes. This separation allows legislation to be technology-neutral while standards provide the technical detail.

The EU AI Act follows this model. Article 40 empowers the Commission to issue standardisation requests to European Standards Organisations (ESOs) — CEN, CENELEC, and ETSI — asking them to develop harmonised standards for specific requirements. Once those standards are developed and cited in the Official Journal, they create a "presumption of conformity": an AI system that complies with the harmonised standard is presumed to meet the corresponding EU AI Act requirements.

The presumption of conformity is rebuttable — it can be challenged — but it significantly reduces the burden of proving compliance. For market surveillance authorities, demonstrating non-conformity with a standardised system requires more evidence than demonstrating non-conformity with a vague regulatory requirement. For providers, compliance with harmonised standards provides legal certainty that a prescriptive compliance approach cannot match.

What conformity presumption means in practice

Without harmonised standards, a provider of high-risk AI must demonstrate that its system meets the EU AI Act's requirements through its own documentation and conformity assessment — and the market surveillance authority makes its own judgement about whether those requirements are met.

With harmonised standards, the provider demonstrates conformity by complying with the standard. The standard specifies exactly what tests, documentation, and processes are required. The conformity assessment body verifies compliance with the standard, and the OJ citation creates the legal presumption.

This matters most for contested compliance questions. When a market surveillance authority challenges a provider's claim that its system meets Article 15's robustness requirements, without a harmonised standard the authority must demonstrate why the provider's approach is inadequate. With a harmonised standard, the question is simpler: does the provider comply with the standard? If not, they must demonstrate that their alternative approach achieves equivalent compliance.

Standards in development (as of September 2026)

The Commission issued standardisation mandates to CEN-CENELEC and ETSI in May 2023. The resulting standards are expected to address the EU AI Act's technical requirements across multiple areas.

No harmonised AI standards have been cited in the Official Journal as of September 2026. The timeline for completion and citation depends on the standardisation process — which typically takes 2–4 years from mandate to OJ citation. Most harmonised AI standards are expected to be available in 2026–2027.

Before harmonised standards arrive: the common specification alternative

In the interim period before harmonised standards are cited, Article 41 of the EU AI Act provides for "common specifications" — technical specifications adopted directly by the Commission via implementing acts. Common specifications are not standards — they are regulatory requirements — but they provide the same legal presumption of conformity.

The Commission may adopt common specifications where harmonised standards are absent, are not sufficient, or where the Commission has concerns about their adequacy. Common specifications are faster to adopt than harmonised standards (no ESO process required) but may be less technically detailed.

For providers who cannot wait for harmonised standards to achieve legal certainty, the practical options are: document compliance carefully with the EU AI Act's requirements directly; use ISO 42001 and the NIST AI RMF as evidence of a systematic approach (they are not harmonised standards but they demonstrate governance); monitor the Common Specifications process; and monitor the JTC 21 standardisation process for early access to draft standards.
