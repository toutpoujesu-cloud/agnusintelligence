Training Data Governance: Legal Basis, Consent and Retention for AI Training Datasets

Every use of personal data in AI training requires a lawful basis. The question of which legal basis applies — and whether consent is ever appropriate — has become one of the most contested questions in AI data governance. This article maps the available legal bases, explains the problems with consent for training data, and addresses the retention and deletion obligations that apply to training datasets.

Jean-Daniel Alexis· 11 September 2026· AI Data Governance pillar· AR-000690

The legal basis requirement

GDPR Article 6 requires that every processing of personal data has a lawful basis. The article lists six bases, of which four are practically relevant for AI training: consent (Article 6(1)(a)), contract (Article 6(1)(b)), legal obligation (Article 6(1)(c)), and legitimate interests (Article 6(1)(f)). Public task (Article 6(1)(e)) applies to public bodies.

The legal basis must be identified and documented before processing begins — not post-hoc. It must be the basis that actually justifies the processing, not the most convenient option. Documenting a legal basis that does not genuinely apply is itself a GDPR violation.

For training data, two further rules apply. First, the legal basis for training data must be the same as, or compatible with, the legal basis under which the data was originally collected. A dataset of customer service conversations collected under a service agreement cannot automatically be used to train an AI model — the original purpose was service delivery, not model training, and using the data for a new purpose requires a compatibility assessment or a new legal basis. Second, for special categories of personal data (health, biometric, religious belief, etc.), Article 9 requires an additional legal basis on top of Article 6.

Why consent is rarely appropriate for AI training

The intuitive response to AI training data governance is: "get consent." This approach has four significant problems that make it impractical for most AI training contexts.

Problem 1: Consent must be specific. GDPR requires that consent is specific to a defined purpose. "For AI training purposes" is not specific enough — it does not tell the individual what the AI will be used for, what features of their data will be used, or what decisions the AI will inform. A consent formulation specific enough to be valid is likely to deter most individuals from consenting.

Problem 2: Consent can be withdrawn. When consent is the legal basis and an individual withdraws consent, their data must be erased and — on the most demanding interpretation — the model must be retrained without their data. For large-scale training datasets, this creates a technical and operational obligation that most organisations cannot fulfil.

Problem 3: Power imbalance. GDPR Article 7 states that consent is not freely given if there is a significant imbalance between the controller and the data subject. In an employment context, asking employees for consent to use their work for AI training is likely invalid because of the power imbalance.

Problem 4: Future use specification. Consent must be for a specified purpose. Consenting to data being used for "AI training" without specifying the model, the use case, or the deployment context is unlikely to satisfy the specificity requirement.

Legitimate interests is the more appropriate legal basis for most commercial AI training — provided the Legitimate Interest Assessment genuinely balances the controller's interests against those of the data subjects and does not override interests that should take precedence.

Retention and deletion obligations

Training datasets are subject to GDPR's storage limitation principle (Article 5(1)(e)): personal data must not be kept longer than necessary for the purpose for which it was processed. For training data, the purpose is training the model — once training is complete, retaining the raw training data requires a separate justification (retraining, audit, compliance).

Most organisations document a separate retention justification for training datasets: the ability to retrain the model with the same data (for consistency and audit trail purposes), or the ability to investigate the model's behaviour in the event of an incident. These are legitimate retention purposes, but they must be documented and time-bounded. "We keep training data indefinitely because we might need it" is not a compliant retention policy.

The deletion obligation applies to the raw training data, not to the trained model. The model's weights are not personal data under current European DPA guidance — they are the output of a statistical process applied to personal data. The personal data (the training records) must be subject to retention limits; the model (the weights) is a separate artefact. This distinction is not settled law, but it represents the current mainstream regulatory position.
