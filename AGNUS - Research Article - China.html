China AI Governance: Three Regulation Strategy, National Standards and Global Ambitions

China has enacted more AI-specific regulations than any other jurisdiction in the world — governing algorithmic recommendations (2022), deepfake synthesis (2022), and generative AI (2023) through three successive regulations from the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC). Understanding China's AI governance framework is essential for organisations operating in China and for understanding the global AI governance landscape.

China's approach: sector-specific binding regulations

China's approach to AI regulation differs fundamentally from both the EU's horizontal AI Act and the US's voluntary framework approach. China has enacted binding regulations targeting specific AI application types — algorithmic recommendation systems, deepfake synthesis technology, and generative AI services — through administrative regulations issued by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) and other authorities.

This sector-specific approach reflects China's governance philosophy: regulate AI applications with significant social impact rapidly and specifically, rather than waiting for a comprehensive horizontal law. China's AI regulations have generally taken effect faster than the EU AI Act, which entered force in August 2024 but applies most provisions from August 2026.

China's AI regulations are also notable for their dual purpose: they address safety and social stability concerns (ensuring AI does not produce content undermining national stability or socialist values) and market regulation concerns (preventing anti-competitive recommendation systems). This dual purpose makes them distinct from purely rights-based Western frameworks.

The three AI regulations

RegulationIn forceKey requirements
Provisions on the Management of Algorithmic Recommendations (算法推荐管理规定) March 2022 Applies to providers of algorithmic recommendation services (content feeds, search ranking, product recommendations) in China.

Key requirements: label algorithmically recommended content; provide explanation of recommendation logic; allow users to opt out of profiling-based recommendations; prohibit using recommendation algorithms to addict users or exploit their behaviours; prohibit differential pricing based on algorithmic profiling; register large-scale providers with CAC.
Provisions on the Management of Deep Synthesis Internet Information Services (深度合成管理规定) January 2023 Applies to providers and users of deepfake synthesis technology — AI-generated images, audio, video, text, and virtual environments.

Key requirements: label AI-generated content; obtain consent before using individuals' biometric data for synthesis; prohibit synthesis that defames individuals or spreads false information; register identities of users; watermark AI-generated content.
Interim Measures for the Management of Generative Artificial Intelligence Services (生成式人工智能服务管理暂行办法) August 2023 Applies to providers of generative AI services to the Chinese public. The most comprehensive of the three regulations.

Key requirements: training data must lawful and representative; outputs must not violate "core socialist values"; implement content safety measures; label AI-generated content; provide human review mechanism; register with CAC for services with "opinion-forming capability"; security assessment for providers with strong societal influence.

PIPL and AI data governance

The Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL), effective November 2021, is China's primary data protection law — often compared to GDPR but with significant differences. For AI deployments in China, several PIPL provisions are particularly relevant:

Automated decision-making (Article 24): Organisations may not engage in automated decision-making that "significantly" impacts personal interests without human verification. Individuals may request explanations of automated decisions and reject them in commercial marketing and risk assessment contexts. This is the closest Chinese equivalent to GDPR Article 22, though narrower.

Training data consent: PIPL requires consent as the primary legal basis for processing personal information, with exceptions for contractual necessity and legitimate interests (narrower than GDPR). Using personal data to train AI models typically requires consent unless another legal basis clearly applies.

Cross-border transfers: China imposes restrictions on cross-border data transfers under PIPL and the Data Security Law (2021). Outbound transfers require a security assessment or approved standard contract clauses for data above certain thresholds. This creates compliance requirements for organisations transferring data between China and other jurisdictions for AI training or inference.

China's international standards strategy

China is actively pursuing leadership in international AI standards through ISO and ITU. At ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 (the committee developing international AI standards), China has significant delegations and has proposed multiple standards. China has proposed AI standards in the ITU that reflect its domestic regulatory framework — potentially influencing international standards in ways that favour the Chinese approach to AI governance.

For organisations involved in international AI standards development, Chinese participation in ISO and ITU AI standardisation is a factor to track. The international AI standards that emerge from these processes will influence the technical specifications for AI governance globally.
