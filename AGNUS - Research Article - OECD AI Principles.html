OECD AI Principles: The Foundation of Global AI Governance Policy

The OECD AI Principles were adopted in May 2019 — the first intergovernmental standard on AI. Updated in 2024 to address generative AI and agentic systems, they have been endorsed by 46 countries including all G7 and G20 members. They informed the EU AI Act, the NIST AI RMF, and dozens of national AI strategies. Understanding them is understanding the conceptual DNA of global AI governance.

Jean-Daniel Alexis· 11 September 2026· Standards pillar· AR-000740

The five OECD AI Principles

The OECD AI Principles define five value-based principles that responsible AI should satisfy, and five complementary policy recommendations for governments. The principles apply to AI actors throughout the AI lifecycle — not just developers or deployers.

1
Inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being
AI should benefit people and the planet. AI actors should steer away from AI that undermines human rights, democratic values, or environmental sustainability. This principle grounds AI governance in its ultimate purpose — AI is not neutral technology; it is developed and deployed in pursuit of human ends.
2
Human-centred values and fairness
AI should respect rule of law, human rights, democratic values, and diversity. AI should not perpetuate or amplify discrimination. Specific mention of privacy, fair competition, and consumer protection. This principle maps most directly to the EU AI Act's prohibited practices (Article 5) and its requirements for fairness and non-discrimination.
3
Transparency and explainability
AI actors should commit to transparency and responsible disclosure about AI systems. People affected by AI outputs should be able to understand AI-assisted decisions and challenge them where appropriate. This principle maps to EU AI Act Article 13 (transparency for high-risk AI) and Article 50 (transparency obligations for certain AI outputs).
4
Robustness, security and safety
AI systems should be technically robust and safe throughout their lifecycle. AI actors should apply a systematic risk management approach. This principle maps to EU AI Act Article 9 (risk management), Article 15 (accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity), and the NIST AI RMF's four functions.
5
Accountability
AI actors should be accountable for the proper functioning of AI systems and for the respect of applicable norms. This includes traceability (sufficient records to investigate incidents) and auditability. Maps to EU AI Act Article 72 (post-market monitoring), Article 73 (incident reporting), and the NIST AI RMF MANAGE function.

Legal weight: principles vs. regulations

The OECD AI Principles are not legally binding. They are a soft law instrument — a recommendation from an intergovernmental organisation to its member governments. They create no enforceable rights or obligations for AI developers or deployers.

Their legal significance is indirect: they influenced the EU AI Act, which is legally binding. The EU AI Act's recitals reference OECD AI Principles explicitly. The NIST AI RMF, which is a US government framework, was designed to be compatible with the OECD principles. National AI strategies in Australia, Canada, Japan, South Korea, and the UK all reference the OECD principles as their normative foundation.

For AI governance practitioners, the principles matter as a reference framework for assessing whether a governance approach is internationally recognised and aligned with the regulatory direction across multiple jurisdictions. A governance programme that satisfies the five principles is on solid conceptual ground in most regulated markets.

The 2024 update: agentic AI and generative AI

The OECD updated its AI Principles in May 2024 to address developments not anticipated in 2019 — specifically, foundation models, generative AI, and agentic AI systems. The 2024 update does not change the five principles but adds significant clarification on their application in these contexts.

The 2024 update explicitly addresses multi-actor responsibility in AI systems — the question of who is accountable when an AI system is built by one party, distributed by another, and deployed by a third. It introduces "AI actors" as a concept that applies throughout the AI lifecycle, and clarifies that accountability attaches to actors at each stage rather than only to the final deployer.

The 2024 update also addresses systemic risk — the risk that very large AI systems (foundation models, general-purpose AI) pose risks to society beyond those addressed by product-level governance. This concept was subsequently incorporated into the EU AI Act's GPAI model provisions (Articles 51–55).
