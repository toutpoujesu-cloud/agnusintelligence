Data Minimisation in AI Systems: Why Using Less Data Is Better Governance

GDPR Article 5(1)(c) requires personal data to be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary." For AI systems, this principle applies at training, fine-tuning, inference, and monitoring — each stage at which personal data may be collected and used. This article maps how data minimisation works at each stage, how it interacts with model performance requirements, and what techniques enable AI systems to achieve both.

Jean-Daniel Alexis· 11 September 2026· AI Data Governance pillar· AR-000670

The data minimisation principle

Data minimisation is not a suggestion to use less data for its own sake. It is a requirement that the data used is adequate (sufficient for its purpose), relevant (connected to the purpose), and limited (not more than necessary). All three elements must be satisfied simultaneously.

For AI systems, "adequate" means the training dataset must be large and representative enough for the model to perform its intended task reliably. "Relevant" means the features used for training and inference must have a genuine causal or statistical relationship to the outcome being predicted. "Limited" means personal data should not be included in the training set or inference pipeline unless it contributes materially to performance on the intended task.

The last element is where AI systems most commonly fail data minimisation. The historical practice of "more data is better" in machine learning led to datasets that include personal attributes — age, gender, location, purchasing history — that improve model performance marginally but create significant data governance obligations. The governance question is whether that marginal improvement justifies the data governance cost and risk.

The tension between minimisation and performance

There is a genuine tension between data minimisation and model performance. More data generally means better performance. More granular personal data often means better predictions. The tension cannot be dissolved by regulation or governance — it must be navigated with judgment.

The key governance question is: what is the marginal performance benefit of each additional personal data attribute, and does that benefit justify the data governance cost? This is not a purely technical question — it is a product and governance decision that should be documented in the risk assessment.

For models subject to EU AI Act high-risk classification, the EU AI Act Article 10(3) provides a specific exception: training data may include special categories of personal data (health, biometric, etc.) where "strictly necessary" and subject to appropriate safeguards. The burden of justification for using special category data is high — "it improves performance" is not sufficient. The data controller must demonstrate that the specific sensitive attributes are strictly necessary for the system to achieve its intended purpose, and that no alternative approach would achieve the same purpose without using that data.

Privacy-preserving techniques that enable minimisation

Differential privacy adds calibrated noise to training data or model outputs, providing mathematical guarantees that individual records cannot be extracted from the model. It allows training on sensitive datasets while limiting the information any individual's data contributes to the model. Google, Apple, and the US Census Bureau have deployed differential privacy in production systems.

Federated learning trains models across distributed devices without centralising the raw data. Each device trains locally and sends model updates (not data) to a central aggregator. The raw personal data never leaves the device. Apple uses federated learning for next-word prediction on iOS keyboards.

Synthetic data generation creates artificial datasets that have similar statistical properties to real personal data without containing real individuals' information. When synthetic data is sufficiently high-quality, it can substitute for personal data in training pipelines — enabling training without processing personal data at all.

Feature selection and ablation systematically removes features from training data and measures the performance impact. Features that do not materially affect performance should be removed regardless of privacy status. Features that improve performance on protected characteristics should be evaluated for legal compatibility before inclusion.
