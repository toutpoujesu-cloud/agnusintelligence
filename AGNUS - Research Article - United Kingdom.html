United Kingdom AI Governance: Post-Brexit Approach, AI Safety Institute and Regulatory Framework

The United Kingdom has positioned itself as a global hub for AI safety research while deliberately avoiding binding AI-specific legislation. Its sector-based, pro-innovation approach contrasts with the EU's horizontal regulatory model — creating a governance divergence that organisations operating across both markets must navigate.

The UK's approach: sector-led, no horizontal AI law

The UK's 2023 AI White Paper established a principles-based framework rather than legislation. The five principles — Safety, Security and Robustness; Appropriate Transparency and Explainability; Fairness; Accountability and Governance; Contestability and Redress — are to be applied by existing sector regulators (FCA, CMA, ICO, Ofcom, HSE) rather than through a new AI-specific regime.

This approach deliberately mirrors the EU's OECD AI Principles inheritance but avoids horizontal legislation. The UK government argued that sector-specific regulation would be more responsive, less prescriptive, and better adapted to the rapidly evolving AI landscape. Critics have argued that without horizontal legislation, vulnerable uses of AI in sectors where no strong regulator exists (entertainment, recruitment, consumer products) remain ungoverned.

The UK AI Opportunities Action Plan (January 2026) maintained this principles-based approach while announcing significant public investment in AI infrastructure — suggesting that the government's primary priority is growing the UK AI sector rather than creating a comprehensive governance regime. A consultation on AI legislation was announced for 2026 but had not concluded as of September 2026.

The AI Safety Institute (now AISI)

The UK AI Safety Institute (AISI), established in November 2023 and subsequently renamed the AI Security Institute, is one of the most significant AI governance institutions in the world — not because of any regulatory authority it holds, but because of its technical research capacity and its position in the international AI safety network.

AISI focuses specifically on frontier AI models — the largest and most capable AI systems. It has conducted evaluations of models from major AI developers including Google DeepMind, Anthropic, and OpenAI, publishing findings on model capabilities and potential risks. Unlike most regulatory bodies, AISI has pre-deployment access to models under voluntary agreements with developers.

AISI operates the AI Safety Summit outcomes framework and collaborates with equivalent institutions in the US (US AI Safety Institute at NIST), the EU (AI Office), and Japan. This international network makes AISI influential beyond the UK's domestic regulatory perimeter — its evaluations inform decisions by developers and regulators globally.

The UK-EU relationship: divergence and adequacy

Post-Brexit, the UK's AI governance regime is diverging from the EU's. The UK has no EU AI Act equivalent and has stated no intention to adopt one. UK GDPR is substantively identical to EU GDPR but is now a separate instrument that can be amended independently by the UK Parliament.

The EU-UK adequacy decision for data transfers, adopted June 2021, is valid until June 2025 (renewal expected). Organisations transferring personal data between the EU and UK operate under this adequacy framework — but if it lapses or is revoked, the implications for AI systems that transfer data between the two jurisdictions would be significant.

Organisations operating in both markets face a dual compliance requirement: EU AI Act obligations for EU market deployments, and UK sector-regulator expectations for UK deployments. In practice, the EU AI Act's more demanding requirements tend to drive compliance in both markets — an organisation that meets EU AI Act standards will typically also satisfy UK regulatory expectations, but not vice versa.
