EU Harmonised Standards for AI: What They Are and When They Will Matter

Published 11 Sep 2026
Reading time 13 min
Pillar Standards
Source EU AI Act Art. 40, 41 · CEN/CENELEC JTC 21 · EC Standardisation Request

Harmonised standards are the EU's mechanism for translating regulatory requirements into technical specifications that industry can implement. For the EU AI Act, harmonised standards are not yet available — but when they are published, compliance with them will create a legal presumption of conformity with the corresponding AI Act requirements. This changes the compliance calculus significantly. Understanding where harmonised standards stand, what they will cover, and when they will arrive is essential for any organisation planning its AI Act compliance programme.

What a harmonised standard is

A harmonised standard is a European standard (EN) developed by a European standards body — CEN, CENELEC, or ETSI — at the request of the European Commission, published in the Official Journal of the European Union, and aligned to the requirements of specific EU legislation. When an organisation demonstrates conformity with a harmonised standard, it benefits from a presumption of conformity with the corresponding requirements of the legislation the standard is harmonised to.

This presumption of conformity is a legal mechanism with practical consequences. It means a conformity assessment body assessing compliance with the EU AI Act can rely on the harmonised standard as evidence of compliance with the requirements it covers, without requiring the applicant to demonstrate compliance through alternative means. It reduces the burden of proof for the organisation and increases predictability in the assessment process.

Harmonised standards are voluntary — an organisation is not required to comply with them. But the alternative — demonstrating conformity with the regulatory requirements through means other than a harmonised standard — is typically more costly, less certain, and more dependent on individual interpretation by the conformity assessment body.

The Commission's standardisation request to CEN/CENELEC

In May 2023, the European Commission issued a formal standardisation request to CEN and CENELEC — the European standards bodies for non-electrical and electrotechnical standards respectively — to develop harmonised standards in support of the EU AI Act. The request was assigned to CEN/CENELEC JTC 21, the Joint Technical Committee on Artificial Intelligence, which had been established in 2021 and already had a programme of AI standards work underway.

The standardisation request covers the full set of technical requirements for high-risk AI under the EU AI Act, organised into the following thematic areas: risk management systems; data and data governance; technical documentation; transparency and provision of information; human oversight; accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity; and quality management systems. For each area, CEN/CENELEC JTC 21 may either develop a new standard, adapt an existing standard, or reference an ISO standard.

No harmonised standards, no presumption — yet. As of September 2026, no harmonised standards for the EU AI Act have been published in the Official Journal of the European Union. The presumption of conformity mechanism is not yet available. This means that organisations seeking to demonstrate conformity with the EU AI Act's technical requirements must do so through alternative means — their own technical documentation, independent assessment, or (for high-risk AI) through notified body certification. The situation will change as standards are published — monitor the OJEU for the publication of harmonised AI standards.

What to do while waiting for harmonised standards

The absence of harmonised standards does not mean organisations have no technical reference points. The following existing standards are relevant to EU AI Act compliance and are likely to inform the harmonised standards when they are published.

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 — AI management systems. Likely to be adopted or adapted as the primary management system standard for EU AI Act compliance. Organisations implementing ISO 42001 now are building a foundation that will translate well to harmonised standard compliance.

ISO/IEC 23894:2023 — AI risk management. Provides a framework for AI risk management that aligns with the EU AI Act's Article 9 requirements. Will inform the CEN/CENELEC risk management technical report.

ISO/IEC 22989:2022 — AI concepts and terminology. The foundational definitions standard. Already cited in the EU AI Act's recitals and relevant to Article 3 definitions.

ISO/IEC TR 24027:2021 — Bias in AI systems and AI-aided decision-making. Relevant to Article 9(7) bias monitoring requirements for high-risk AI.

NIST AI RMF 1.0 (2023) — Not a European standard, but widely used and compatible with the EU AI Act's risk management approach. Particularly useful for organisations operating in both the US and EU.

Common specifications as an alternative

Article 41 of the EU AI Act provides for the European Commission to issue common specifications — technical standards developed directly by the Commission rather than by the standards bodies — in cases where harmonised standards are not available or are inadequate. Common specifications have the same presumption of conformity effect as harmonised standards and are published as Commission implementing acts.

Common specifications are intended as a fallback, not a first resort. The Commission has indicated it will use this mechanism sparingly. However, for areas where harmonised standards are significantly delayed — which, given the current timelines, includes most of the high-risk AI technical requirements — common specifications may arrive before harmonised standards in some areas.

The practical implication for compliance programmes. Organisations planning EU AI Act compliance programmes should not wait for harmonised standards before acting. The regulatory requirements are in force; the absence of harmonised standards does not create a compliance holiday. It means organisations must build their compliance cases through alternative means — which in practice means aligning to existing international standards (ISO 42001, ISO 23894), documenting that alignment in technical documentation, and being prepared to defend that documentation to a notified body or market surveillance authority. When harmonised standards are published, the compliance case will need to be reviewed and updated, but organisations that have already built structured governance frameworks will find that transition manageable.
