AI Agent Incident Response: What to Do When an Agent Behaves Unexpectedly

Every AI agent deployment will eventually produce an unexpected behaviour — an action outside scope, an anomalous output, a policy violation, or a system compromise. Incident response for AI agents differs from classical IT incident response in one critical way: the agent's decision-making process is not directly inspectable. This article sets out a structured response framework for the most common incident types.

Jean-Daniel Alexis · 11 September 2026 · AI Agents pillar · AR-000540

What counts as an AI agent incident

An AI agent incident is any event in which an agent's behaviour deviates from its governed operating parameters in a way that has, or could have, a material consequence. This includes:

Scope violation: The agent takes an action outside its Mission Contract — accessing a resource it is not authorised to access, performing an action type not in its capability set, or exceeding an approval threshold without obtaining approval.

Policy violation: The agent takes an action that its policy engine should have blocked — suggesting either a policy configuration error, a policy evaluation failure, or an attempt to circumvent the policy engine.

Anomalous behaviour: The agent's behaviour changes in a way that is not explained by an instruction change — volume anomalies, unexpected resource access patterns, outputs that differ from historical norms.

Suspected compromise: There is reason to believe the agent's context has been manipulated — through prompt injection, poisoned data, or a compromised tool call response. This is a particularly serious incident type because a compromised agent may propagate malicious instructions to other agents.

Consequential error: The agent produces an output that causes material harm — financial loss, data exposure, incorrect decision, or harm to a person — regardless of whether a policy was technically violated.

The six incident response phases

1
Detection
Identify that an incident has occurred or is occurring. For AI agents, detection typically comes from: automated policy engine alerts, audit log anomaly detection, human review of outputs, or an affected party reporting harm.
  • Log the detection event with timestamp, source, and initial description
  • Assign a severity classification and incident ID immediately
  • Notify the named human sponsor
2
Containment
Stop the immediate harm. For AI agents, containment means stopping or restricting the agent — not investigating root cause. Containment precedes investigation.
  • For Critical severity: terminate the agent session immediately
  • For High severity: restrict to read-only or suspend until investigation
  • Preserve all context: do not clear logs, memory, or tool call histories
  • If other agents may be affected by a compromised context, review their recent actions
3
Evidence preservation
Lock the evidence before it can change or be overwritten. This step is time-critical — session logs, audit records, and tool call histories may be subject to retention policies that delete them.
  • Export and hash the full audit log for the affected session
  • Export the agent's Mission Contract at the time of the incident
  • Export all policy engine evaluation records for the affected time window
  • If external systems were affected, request preservation of their logs
4
Investigation
Determine what happened, how it happened, and why the governance controls did not prevent it. For AI agents, this requires reconstructing the event sequence from logs — the agent's reasoning process cannot be directly inspected.
  • Reconstruct the action sequence from audit logs with timestamps
  • Identify the specific policy or control that failed or was absent
  • Determine whether the behaviour was within the Mission Contract
  • Assess whether prompt injection or context manipulation may have occurred
5
Notification
Determine whether external notification is required — to regulators, affected individuals, or supervisory authorities. See the notification obligations table below.
  • Assess whether GDPR Article 33 (supervisory authority) or Article 34 (data subjects) apply
  • Assess whether EU AI Act Article 73 (serious incident reporting) applies
  • Document the notification decision and its reasoning, whether or not notification is required
6
Remediation and review
Fix the root cause, update the governance controls, and restart the agent under the corrected framework.
  • Update the Mission Contract to close the identified gap
  • Update or add policy rules that would have prevented the incident
  • Review similar deployments for the same gap
  • Do not restart the agent until the human sponsor has approved the corrected contract

Serious incident involving high-risk AI — death or serious health impact 15 days Market surveillance authority (EU AI Act Art. 73)
Serious incident involving high-risk AI — other serious harm 2 days Market surveillance authority (EU AI Act Art. 73)
Serious incident in financial services AI Sector-specific Financial supervisory authority (sector regulation)

The EU AI Act's serious incident definition covers incidents that directly or indirectly lead to death, serious harm to health, significant property damage, or fundamental rights violations. For most AI agent incidents in commercial settings, the notification threshold will be GDPR Article 33 (personal data breach) rather than the AI Act. Both may apply simultaneously.

Evidence preservation and the audit trail

The most common failure in AI agent incident response is evidence loss. When an agent session ends, its context — the full sequence of inputs, tool calls, and outputs — may be deleted or overwritten. If the incident is identified after the session has ended, critical evidence may already be gone.

The mitigation is pre-incident: audit logs must be written to append-only storage with a retention period longer than the expected interval between incidents. Logs that cannot be written to (only read from and audited) cannot be tampered with after the fact — which matters when the logs are submitted to a regulatory authority or used in litigation.

The minimum evidence that should be preserved for every incident includes: the full action log for the affected session with timestamps; the agent's Mission Contract at the time of the incident; the policy engine evaluation records; any external system logs from systems the agent interacted with; and any outputs the agent produced. These should be exported, hashed (SHA-256 minimum), and stored in a location the affected agent cannot access.
