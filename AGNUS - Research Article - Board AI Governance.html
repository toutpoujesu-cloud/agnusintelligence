Board AI Governance: What the Board Needs to Own, Decide, and Report

Published 11 Sep 2026
Reading time 14 min
Pillar AI Governance
Source EU AI Act Art. 26 · UK Corporate Governance Code · NIST AI RMF

The board of directors is not expected to understand how large language models work. It is expected to understand what risks AI creates for the organisation, to make decisions that cannot be delegated, and to ensure that adequate governance structures exist. That is a much harder set of requirements — and most boards are not meeting it.

Why AI governance has become a board matter

The EU AI Act, fully applicable from August 2026, creates legal obligations for the organisations that deploy AI systems. Those obligations are not assigned to the AI team, the CTO, or the Chief Risk Officer — they are assigned to the organisation as a legal entity, which means ultimate accountability rests with its board. Article 26 of the EU AI Act requires deployers to implement appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure human oversight of high-risk AI systems. The UK Financial Conduct Authority has made clear that AI governance is an aspect of operational resilience, for which senior managers are personally accountable under the Senior Managers and Certification Regime. The New York City Local Law 144 requires audits of automated employment decision tools — a requirement that carries legal risk at the organisational level.

This is not the first time boards have faced an entirely new risk category. Twenty years ago, boards were not expected to understand cybersecurity architecture. Today, a board that did not have a cybersecurity briefing at least annually, did not have a named board-level owner for cyber risk, and did not ask what the organisation's breach response plan was, would be considered negligent. AI governance is at the same early inflection point. The question is not whether it will become a board-level issue — it already is in regulated sectors. The question is how quickly boards in other sectors recognise this.

Three categories of board responsibility

Board responsibility for AI governance falls into three distinct categories: ownership (what the board must directly own), decision-making (what cannot be delegated below board level), and oversight (what the board must receive, review, and challenge). Getting this taxonomy right matters — boards that try to own too much become operationally involved and lose strategic perspective; boards that delegate too much lose the governance control the law requires.

What the board must own directly

Board ownership
AI risk appetite
The board, not the executive, must set the organisation's AI risk appetite — the answer to: how much AI risk are we willing to accept? This includes the organisation's position on autonomous AI decision-making in high-stakes processes, its approach to AI in customer-facing contexts, and the conditions under which AI use will be paused or stopped. Without a board-set risk appetite, AI governance decisions made by management have no reference point.
Board ownership
AI governance framework approval
The organisation's AI governance framework — its policy for how AI systems are acquired, deployed, monitored, and decommissioned — must be approved by the board, not only by the executive. This signals to management, regulators, and third parties that AI governance has board-level commitment. It also creates board accountability: if the framework is inadequate and harm results, the board approved it.
Board ownership
Named board-level AI governance owner
There must be a named board member — not a committee, not a shared responsibility — who is accountable for AI governance at board level. In practice, this is typically the chair of the audit committee, the senior independent director, or a board member with a technology background. The named owner does not manage the AI governance programme day-to-day; they ensure it exists, is resourced, and is functioning.

What the board must receive, review, and challenge

The following items should appear in board packs or board committee reports at the stated frequency. The board's job is not to accept management reports unchallenged — it is to ask the questions that management's own reporting will not surface.

The five questions every board member should be able to answer

The following five questions are the minimum standard for informed board-level AI governance. A board member who cannot answer these questions is not equipped to provide meaningful oversight.

1. What AI systems does our organisation operate? The answer requires reference to the AI inventory. If the inventory does not exist or is not current, this question cannot be answered — and that answer is itself the most important governance finding.

2. Which of our AI systems are high-risk under the EU AI Act? High-risk systems have specific obligations under Article 26. Boards in organisations operating in financial services, employment, education, critical infrastructure, or law enforcement should be particularly alert to this question.

3. Who is accountable for AI governance in our organisation, and what authority do they have? There must be a named person (not a team, not a function) who is accountable for the AI governance programme. The board should know who that is and what they can do without seeking permission from elsewhere.

4. What happened in our most recent AI incident, and how did we respond? If the answer is "we have not had an AI incident," the follow-up question is: do we have a mechanism that would detect one? Most AI incidents are not announced — they are discovered through monitoring, customer complaints, or external reporting.

5. Are we in a position to meet our EU AI Act obligations for our high-risk systems by the applicable deadline? For systems already deployed when the AI Act became applicable, the question is about current compliance. For systems being newly developed or procured, the question is about readiness.

What good looks like at board level. A board that is functioning well on AI governance has: an approved AI policy; a named board-level owner; a current AI inventory presented for approval annually; a quarterly incident report that includes near-misses, not only confirmed incidents; a biannual regulatory update; and a culture in which any board member who identifies a potential AI governance concern raises it without waiting for the next scheduled report. None of this requires the board to understand how transformers work.

Building board capability without turning directors into technologists

The most common objection to board-level AI governance is that board members are not technical enough to provide meaningful oversight. This objection misunderstands what board oversight requires. A board member overseeing a pharmaceutical company does not need to understand organic chemistry. A board member overseeing a bank does not need to understand derivative pricing models. What they need to understand is: what are the material risks, who is accountable for managing them, what would indicate that those risks are not being managed, and what decisions require board-level involvement.

The same applies to AI. Board capability for AI governance is built through three mechanisms: regular structured briefings (not technical deep-dives, but risk and compliance briefings delivered in plain language); one-to-one engagement between the named board AI governance owner and the organisation's AI governance lead; and external input — whether from an AI governance adviser, from peer networks, or from regulatory guidance. None of these require a board member to learn to code.

The test of adequate board capability is not whether board members can explain how a neural network works. It is whether they can ask the right questions, recognise a materially inadequate answer, and take appropriate action when the governance picture they receive does not reflect the organisation's actual state.
