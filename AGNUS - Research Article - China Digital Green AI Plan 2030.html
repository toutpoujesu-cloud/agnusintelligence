Seven Chinese authorities issue a 2026–2030 implementation plan for AI resource efficiency and lifecycle disclosure

China's Cyberspace Administration and six other central authorities have published a joint plan requiring lifecycle resource-consumption assessments for generative AI, disclosure of large-model resource utilisation, and a 2030 policy horizon for green AI infrastructure.

Source: Cyberspace Administration of China — official joint plan publication (SRC-0102). The plan is in Chinese; this record is based on the verified translated summary in the AGNUS intelligence handoff. Implementing regulations and standards have not yet been published. This record will be updated as implementing guidance is issued. AGNUS does not provide legal advice.
Contents

    What happened

    On 4 September 2026, the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) published a joint implementation plan on behalf of seven central authorities, covering the coordination of digitalisation and greenisation — the Chinese policy agenda of simultaneously advancing digital transformation and carbon reduction. The plan has a 2026–2030 policy horizon.

    The plan is titled "Implementation Plan for Promoting the Coordinated Transformation and Development of Digitalisation and Greenisation (2026–2030)" and was jointly issued by CAC together with six other ministries and agencies, including (based on standard cross-ministry governance practice in this area) the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, the National Development and Reform Commission, and the National Energy Administration.

    The plan addresses AI specifically in the context of its resource consumption — training compute, inference power, data-centre energy — and creates a policy framework for measuring, disclosing, and reducing AI's environmental footprint.

    What the plan requires for AI

    Three elements of the plan are directly relevant to AI providers and operators:

    1. Lifecycle resource-consumption assessment

    The plan calls for the establishment of a lifecycle resource-consumption assessment system for generative AI. This means that the resource impact of AI systems — from training through deployment and inference — must be measured and assessed as a lifecycle whole, not just at individual operational stages. This is a more demanding framework than point-in-time energy-consumption reporting.

    2. Large-model resource-utilisation disclosure

    The plan includes guidance for providers to disclose large-model resource utilisation — covering energy consumption, carbon footprint, and computing resource intensity. This disclosure obligation targets frontier AI model providers specifically. The precise format and verification requirements for disclosure will be set out in subsequent implementing guidance.

    3. Infrastructure and deployment efficiency

    The plan promotes a set of technical approaches to reducing AI resource consumption:

    Training efficiency

    Promoting efficient training methods that reduce compute intensity per model capability unit

    Inference optimisation

    Reducing per-query energy consumption through optimised inference architectures

    Model compression

    Encouraging lightweight model variants and quantisation to reduce deployment resource requirements

    Green infrastructure

    Promoting lower-power hardware and greener computing facilities for AI workloads

    Why this matters

    China's plan represents the most detailed national AI resource-efficiency framework published to date by a major AI-deploying government. While the EU AI Act's delegated acts address energy transparency for GPAI models, China's plan goes further in requiring lifecycle assessment and in promoting specific technical approaches to efficiency.

    For international AI operators with China operations, the plan creates expectations — and likely forthcoming mandatory requirements — around how AI systems are deployed, how their resource consumption is measured, and what is disclosed to regulators and the public. The 2030 horizon means that implementing regulations and standards will be issued progressively over the plan period.

    Disclosure trajectory: The plan's disclosure guidance is currently advisory. However, China's regulatory pattern — as demonstrated with the generative AI measures and the deep synthesis regulations — is to progress from guidance to mandatory requirement within 12–24 months. Providers should treat the disclosure framework as the likely shape of forthcoming mandatory rules, not merely aspirational policy.

    What organisations should watch

    • Implementing guidance from CAC and MIIT specifying disclosure formats, verification requirements, and assessment methodology
    • National standards from the Standardisation Administration of China (SAC) and TC260 on AI energy consumption measurement
    • Whether the lifecycle assessment requirement extends to foreign AI services accessed by Chinese users
    • EU GPAI model energy transparency requirements under the AI Act — for comparison with China's approach
    • Any trade or procurement implications of the green AI framework for government and state-enterprise AI procurement
