Cross-Border AI Data Transfers: GDPR Chapter V and What It Means for Global AI Deployments

Most commercial AI deployments involve cross-border data transfers. Training data uploaded to a cloud provider's servers in a third country. Inference requests sent to an API hosted outside the EU. Model weights stored on infrastructure spanning multiple jurisdictions. Each of these may constitute a transfer of personal data, triggering GDPR Chapter V obligations.

Jean-Daniel Alexis· 11 September 2026· AI Data Governance pillar· AR-000700

When does a cross-border transfer occur in AI?

A cross-border transfer under GDPR Chapter V occurs when personal data is transmitted to, made accessible to, or stored in a third country (a country outside the EEA) or an international organisation. For AI deployments, transfers typically occur at three points:

Training data upload: When personal data is uploaded to a cloud provider for training, the data may be stored in or processed in third countries depending on the provider's infrastructure. Most major cloud providers maintain EEA data residency options, but the default infrastructure often spans global regions.

Inference API calls: When a deployed application sends personal data to an AI inference API (such as an OpenAI or Anthropic API endpoint), the request and any personal data it contains is transmitted to the provider's servers, which may be located in a third country.

Model weight storage and access: When AI model weights are stored in third-country infrastructure, and those weights embed patterns learned from personal data, whether this constitutes a "transfer" of personal data is contested. Current European DPA guidance does not treat model weights as personal data — but this position may evolve.

The transfer mechanisms available

Transfer mechanismHow it worksPractical use for AIStability
Adequacy decision (Art. 45) The European Commission has determined that the third country provides an adequate level of data protection. No additional safeguard required. Transfers to the UK (under the UK adequacy decision), Japan, South Korea, Canada, New Zealand, and other adequacy countries require no additional safeguard. Stable — while in force. Adequacy decisions can be revoked.
Standard Contractual Clauses (Art. 46) Commission-adopted contract terms that provide appropriate safeguards. Used for transfers to third countries without adequacy decisions. Most common mechanism for transfers to US-based AI providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft). The 2021 SCCs include a module for controller-to-processor transfers. Conditional — SCCs require a Transfer Impact Assessment (TIA) to verify the destination country's law does not undermine them.
EU-US Data Privacy Framework (Art. 45) Adequacy decision adopted July 2023 for transfers to certified US organisations. Replaced Privacy Shield, which was invalidated by Schrems II in 2020. Simplifies transfers to US AI providers that are DPF-certified. Most major US AI providers have obtained DPF certification. Under scrutiny — challenges before the CJEU are anticipated, as with Privacy Shield and Safe Harbour before it.
Binding Corporate Rules (Art. 47) Approved data protection policies applicable within a multinational corporate group. Relevant for organisations deploying AI across internal group entities in different countries. Stable — once approved, valid for intra-group transfers indefinitely.
Derogations (Art. 49) Specific circumstances where a transfer is permitted without an adequacy decision or safeguards — consent, contract necessity, vital interests, public interest, legal claims, compelling legitimate interests. May apply to one-off AI inference calls by individuals who consent. Not appropriate for systematic, large-scale transfers. Narrow — derogations are exceptions, not the rule. Systematic reliance on Art. 49 for AI transfers is not compliant.

Practical implications for AI deployments

Mapping your transfer flows. Before deploying any AI system that processes personal data, map every point at which that data leaves the EEA: training data uploads, inference API calls, logging and monitoring data, third-party components. Each transfer point requires a lawful transfer mechanism.

Reviewing AI provider DPAs. Most AI providers offer Data Processing Agreements (DPAs) that address transfer mechanisms. Review whether the DPA relies on SCCs, the DPF, adequacy, or a combination. Verify that the provider's DPF certification covers the categories of data you will transfer. Verify that the SCC modules match your relationship (controller-processor vs. controller-controller).

Transfer Impact Assessments. When SCCs are the transfer mechanism, a Transfer Impact Assessment (TIA) is required to verify that the destination country's law does not prevent the SCCs from operating effectively. For transfers to the US under SCCs (rather than the DPF), the TIA must address the impact of US surveillance legislation (FISA Section 702, EO 12333) on the protection the SCCs provide.

Data residency options. Many major cloud and AI providers offer EU data residency options — infrastructure dedicated to the EEA where personal data does not leave the region. These options eliminate the cross-border transfer risk for data at rest and in processing, though they often come at a cost premium and may limit access to the latest model versions.

Key adequacy decisions relevant to AI deployments

UK: The European Commission adopted an adequacy decision for the UK in June 2021. This simplifies transfers between EEA and UK entities. The decision is valid until June 2025 and must be renewed — renewal is expected but not guaranteed.

US: The EU-US Data Privacy Framework (DPF) adequacy decision was adopted in July 2023. Transfers to DPF-certified US organisations are straightforward. CJEU challenges are anticipated — organisations should maintain SCC coverage as a fallback.

Japan and South Korea: Both have adequacy decisions, simplifying transfers to AI providers and infrastructure in these jurisdictions. South Korea's adequacy decision was adopted in December 2021.

No adequacy decision: China, India, most of Southeast Asia, most of Latin America, and most of Africa do not have adequacy decisions. Transfers to these regions require SCCs or BCRs — and a TIA that addresses the destination country's legal framework.
