United States AI Governance: Executive Orders, NIST and the Emerging Federal Framework

The United States has not enacted a federal AI Act. Instead, AI governance operates through executive orders (which shift with each administration), agency guidance, voluntary commitments from major AI developers, and an increasingly active state-level legislative landscape. This article maps the current US AI governance environment and what it means for organisations operating in the US market.

The federal AI governance landscape

The United States does not have a federal AI Act. AI governance at the federal level operates through three channels: executive orders (which can be issued and revoked by any administration); existing statutory authority (which various agencies — FTC, EEOC, CFPB, SEC, FDA — use to apply existing laws to AI deployments); and voluntary frameworks (primarily the NIST AI RMF and voluntary commitments by AI developers).

This fragmented approach means that the regulatory exposure of an AI deployer in the US depends heavily on the industry sector (financial services AI is regulated by the FCA; healthcare AI by the FDA; employment AI by the EEOC), the state of operation (California and Colorado have enacted AI-specific laws), and the political administration (executive orders have shifted significantly between administrations).

As of September 2026, the US AI governance landscape is characterised by strong federal investment in AI development and safety research, active agency enforcement of existing laws against AI-enabled harm, and an increasingly active Congress debating AI legislation — but no enacted federal AI Act.

Executive Orders: Biden (2023) and the Trump revision (2025)

President Biden's Executive Order on AI (EO 14110, October 2023) was the most comprehensive federal AI governance action to that point. It required safety testing for frontier AI models, established reporting requirements for developers, directed agencies to develop AI guidelines, and advanced principles for AI use by the federal government.

In January 2025, President Trump revoked EO 14110 and replaced it with a new Executive Order focused on removing barriers to AI development and reducing "excessive" regulation. The Trump order directed agencies to revise their AI guidance to eliminate requirements seen as inconsistent with a pro-growth AI policy. It also directed the removal of the US AI Safety Institute's role in evaluating AI models from private developers.

For organisations subject to both EU and US requirements, this policy shift has practical implications: US federal AI safety requirements that were aligned with EU AI Act obligations under the Biden EO have been reduced, while EU AI Act requirements remain unchanged. The divergence between US and EU AI governance regimes has widened since January 2025.

For operators: what US AI governance means in practice

Sector-specific compliance remains the primary obligation. Financial services AI must comply with FTC Act (unfair practices), ECOA (equal credit opportunity), and CFPB guidance. Healthcare AI must comply with FDA Software as a Medical Device guidance and HIPAA. Employment AI must comply with EEOC guidance on AI in hiring and the ADA. The relevant agency, not a horizontal AI Act, determines the compliance framework.

State law creates a patchwork. Colorado's AI Act took effect February 2026. California's multiple AI disclosure laws are in force. Organisations with customers or employees in multiple states must track and comply with multiple state AI laws simultaneously.

Voluntary commitments have governance implications. Major AI developers (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, Microsoft) have made voluntary commitments to the Biden White House and to the UK AI Safety Institute regarding safety testing, transparency, and incident reporting. These commitments are not legally binding but are publicly stated and may inform regulatory expectations.

EU AI Act creates indirect obligations. Organisations operating in both markets find that EU AI Act compliance drives their global AI governance standards — the US requirements are generally less demanding, so EU-compliant systems typically satisfy US obligations. This is the "Brussels Effect" applied to AI governance.
