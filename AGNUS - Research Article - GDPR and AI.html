GDPR and AI: How Data Protection Law Applies to AI Systems

GDPR applies to AI systems that process personal data at every stage: training data collection, model development, inference, and output generation. The EU AI Act and GDPR are both in force and both apply — compliance with one does not imply compliance with the other. The EDPB’s June 2025 guidelines clarify the interplay. Key friction points: legal basis for training data, purpose limitation, Article 22 automated decision-making, and DPIA / AI Act risk assessment alignment.

Key context
"GDPR and the AI Act are not alternatives. They apply simultaneously. An AI system that processes personal data in high-risk contexts must comply with both frameworks — and where they conflict, the more restrictive provision applies."
AGNUS Research · GDPR Article 22 · EU AI Act Article 10
The GDPR is technology-neutral. It applies to AI systems not because they are AI, but because they process personal data. Every AI system that touches personal data at any stage is a GDPR subject.
Contents

    Builds on AR-000200 (AI Act Explained) and AR-000250 (Deployer Obligations). GDPR and the EU AI Act run in parallel for AI systems that process personal data. This article maps where they overlap, where they conflict, and which takes precedence. The primary source is the EDPB Guidelines on the GDPR/AI Act interplay (June 2025).

    Where GDPR applies to AI

    Two regulations, one AI system

    Legal basis for AI processing

    Legal basis (Article 6 GDPR) AI use case where it may apply Key limitations
    Consent (Article 6(1)(a)) Training data collection where individuals voluntarily provide data with explicit consent to AI training use. Consent must be specific to the AI training purpose. Generic terms-of-service consent for AI training has been found insufficient by multiple DPAs. Consent can be withdrawn; withdrawal removes the legal basis for future processing but may not require model deletion (open question).
    Contract (Article 6(1)(b)) Processing user inputs and generating outputs where the AI service is the contracted service. Covers processing necessary to perform the contract. Cannot extend to training data collection or model improvement beyond the contract scope without additional legal basis.
    Legitimate interests (Article 6(1)(f)) Internal AI model development using employee or customer data where the organisation has a genuine interest and the data subject’s rights do not override. Requires a balancing test (legitimate interests assessment). The interest must be real, the processing necessary, and the data subject’s interests not overriding. DPAs have been skeptical of legitimate interests as a catch-all for AI training. Cannot be used by public authorities in the exercise of their tasks.
    Legal obligation (Article 6(1)(c)) Processing required by law, e.g. compliance monitoring AI where the monitoring obligation is statutory. The obligation must be in Union or Member State law and must specify the processing. Cannot be used to justify AI training as a general compliance activity.
    Public task (Article 6(1)(e)) Public authority AI deployment for tasks carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority. Must be in Union or Member State law. The specific AI deployment must be covered by the task definition. Legitimate interests cannot be used instead.

    Purpose limitation and AI training

    Article 22: Automated decision-making

    DPIAs and the AI Act risk assessment

    Key friction points

    Friction point 1
    Training data and purpose limitation
    Data collected for operational purposes cannot automatically be repurposed for AI training. A fresh legal basis or compatibility assessment is required. Most commercial AI training does not qualify for the scientific research exception.
    Friction point 2
    Erasure rights and trained models
    If a data subject withdraws consent or requests erasure under Article 17, and their personal data was used to train the model, the legal consequences for the trained model are unresolved. Machine unlearning is technically immature. The EDPB guidelines acknowledge this as an open question.
    Friction point 3
    Transparency about AI use
    GDPR Article 13/14 requires informing individuals about automated processing of their data. The EU AI Act Article 50 requires informing individuals when they interact with AI in certain contexts. Both apply; information must cover both the personal data processing and the AI system use.
    Friction point 4
    Special category data in AI systems
    Processing special category data (health, biometric, racial/ethnic origin, political opinions, religion, trade union membership, sexual orientation) requires an Article 9 exception in addition to an Article 6 legal basis. AI systems that infer special category attributes from non-special-category inputs (e.g. inferring health status from spending patterns) may trigger Article 9 requirements without an obvious exception.

    What organizations must do

    • Map every stage of personal data processing in your AI system lifecycle: training data collection, model development, inference inputs, inference outputs, audit logs, and intermediate caches. Establish a legal basis for each stage separately.
    • Conduct a purpose limitation assessment before using any existing dataset for AI training. Document whether the training purpose is compatible with the original collection purpose, and if not, identify the fresh legal basis.
    • For AI systems that make consequential individual decisions (credit, employment, benefits, risk scoring): assess whether Article 22 applies. If it does, implement one of the three permitted conditions, document suitable safeguards (human review, contestation right, explanation), and update privacy notices.
    • Conduct a DPIA before deploying any AI system that is likely to result in high risk to natural persons. For public-sector or essential-services deployers, also conduct a FRIA under Article 27 EU AI Act. Both can be conducted jointly but must be separately documented.
    • Update privacy notices to inform individuals about AI use: what processing occurs, the legal basis, the logic involved in automated decisions, and the rights available (including the right to human review under Article 22 where applicable).
    • Design AI audit trail logs to pseudonymise personal data at collection: retain the audit record but minimise personal data retention. Establish a documented retention period for personal data in logs, consistent with GDPR data minimisation and the audit trail purpose.
    • Verify the DPA position in each Member State where you operate: national DPAs have taken different positions on AI/GDPR issues (particularly on legitimate interests for AI training and the scientific research exception). The EDPB guidelines are the baseline; national guidance may be more restrictive.

    Common mistakes

    Treating EU AI Act conformity assessment as a GDPR compliance exercise is the most common mistake. They are different instruments addressing different risks. A conformity assessment that finds the AI system technically compliant with Articles 9–15 of the EU AI Act says nothing about whether the personal data processing in that system is lawful under GDPR.

    Using legitimate interests as a catch-all legal basis for AI training data is a second mistake. DPAs have been consistently skeptical of this approach. Legitimate interests requires a genuine balancing test; it is not a default fallback when consent is impractical and no other basis applies. The EDPB guidelines do not endorse it as a general basis for commercial AI training.

    Assuming that informing users about AI (under Article 50 EU AI Act) discharges the GDPR transparency obligation is a third mistake. Article 50 requires informing individuals that they are interacting with an AI system. GDPR Articles 13/14 require informing individuals about the personal data processing that AI system performs — legal basis, purposes, retention, rights. Both are required; neither covers the other.

    Treating the FRIA as a substitute for a DPIA is a fourth mistake. A FRIA under Article 27 EU AI Act addresses fundamental rights impacts broadly. A DPIA under Article 35 GDPR addresses data protection risks specifically. Both are required for high-risk AI systems that process personal data; they should be coordinated but not conflated.

    Open questions

    • What GDPR obligations arise when a data subject requests erasure of personal data used to train a model — machine unlearning is technically immature; the EDPB guidelines acknowledge this as unresolved. Different DPAs may take different positions pending further guidance.
    • Whether legitimate interests is an available legal basis for commercial AI training on publicly available data — the EDPB guidelines do not confirm this position; several DPAs (including the Irish DPA in its Meta/LinkedIn enforcement) have taken restrictive positions.
    • How Article 22 applies to AI systems used as decision-support tools where a human nominally takes the decision — if the human decision is substantially shaped by the AI output and has no meaningful independent basis, it may still qualify as ‘solely automated’ processing for Article 22 purposes. The EDPB guidelines note this is context-dependent.
    • Whether the Digital Omnibus amended any EU AI Act provisions that interact with GDPR (Articles 10, 27, 50) — not confirmed on this research pass; verify against the current consolidated text.

    Frequently asked

    FAQ
    Does GDPR apply to AI models that are trained on publicly available data?
    GDPR applies if the publicly available data contains personal data relating to identifiable individuals. The fact that data is publicly available does not remove it from GDPR scope — it may affect the legal basis analysis (particularly legitimate interests) but does not create a blanket exemption. Web-scraped text containing names, email addresses, or other identifiable information is personal data regardless of its public availability.
    FAQ
    Does Article 22 GDPR apply to all AI decision-making?
    No. Article 22 applies to decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal effects or similarly significant effects on individuals. An AI system used to personalise a news feed does not trigger Article 22. An AI system used to determine credit eligibility, screen job applications, or set insurance premiums does. The key threshold is whether the decision produces a legal effect (e.g. contract approval/rejection) or a similarly significant effect (e.g. material impact on the individual’s opportunities, behaviour, or status).
    FAQ
    Is a DPIA required for every AI system?
    No. A DPIA is required for processing that is likely to result in a high risk to natural persons. The GDPR Article 35(3) list of mandatory DPIA scenarios (systematic profiling, large-scale sensitive data, systematic monitoring of public areas) covers many but not all AI deployments. The national DPA’s list of processing types that always require a DPIA (published under Article 35(4)) is the authoritative reference for each Member State. For high-risk AI systems under the EU AI Act that process personal data, the overlap between mandatory DPIA triggers and high-risk AI classifications is large but not complete.
