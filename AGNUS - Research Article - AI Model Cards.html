AI Model Cards and Transparency Documentation: What They Are and Why They Matter

A model card is a short document attached to an AI model that describes its intended use, performance on different data distributions, known limitations, and ethical considerations. Originally proposed by researchers at Google in 2019, model cards have become a de facto standard for AI transparency — and their content maps closely to the EU AI Act's technical documentation requirements for high-risk AI.

Jean-Daniel Alexis· 11 September 2026· AI Audit pillar· AR-000650

What model cards are and why they exist

The model card concept was introduced in a 2019 paper by Mitchell et al. (Google) as a solution to a practical problem: AI models were being deployed in contexts their developers had not intended, with performance characteristics their users did not understand. A model trained on data from one demographic might perform poorly on another. A model evaluated on one task might produce harmful outputs on a different task. Without documentation, deployers had no way to know.

Model cards solve this by standardising what information is attached to a model at release. They are not a guarantee of safety — they are a transparency tool. A model card that honestly documents known performance disparities across demographic groups allows a deployer to make an informed decision about whether the model is appropriate for a specific use case.

The practice has spread significantly since 2019. Major AI providers (Google, Meta, Microsoft, Hugging Face) publish model cards for their foundation models. The EU AI Act's technical documentation requirements make model card-style documentation legally required for high-risk AI — though the Act does not use the term "model card."

What a model card must contain

Model card structure — eight required sections
1. Model details
Model name and version. Organisation that developed it. Date of release. Type of model (classifier, generative, recommendation, etc.). Input and output types. Contact information for questions.
2. Intended use
Primary intended use cases. Intended users. Uses the model is explicitly not designed for (out-of-scope uses). This section sets the boundary within which the model's documented performance claims apply.
3. Training data
Description of training data sources. Known biases in training data. Data collection methodology. Date range of training data (relevant for knowledge cutoff). Data preprocessing applied.
4. Evaluation data
Description of the datasets used for evaluation. Their relationship to the intended deployment context. Known gaps between evaluation data and deployment context.
5. Performance metrics
The metrics used to evaluate performance. Results on the evaluation datasets. Results disaggregated by relevant subgroups (demographic groups, data types, etc.) where applicable.
6. Ethical considerations
Known risks. Known disparities in performance across groups. Potential for misuse. Recommended use limitations based on ethical considerations.
7. Limitations and caveats
Technical limitations. Known failure modes. Conditions under which the model performs poorly. What the model should not be used to decide without additional human review.
8. Recommendations
Specific recommendations for deployers — conditions for appropriate use, monitoring requirements, human oversight mechanisms, and use cases that should be approached with caution.
The original Mitchell et al. (2019) model card specification covers sections 1–8. The EU AI Act Annex IV technical documentation requires equivalent information plus operational considerations not covered by a model card alone.

Mapping model cards to EU AI Act requirements

Model card sectionEU AI Act Annex IV equivalentCovered?
Model detailsAnnex IV(1): General description of the AI systemPartially — Annex IV requires more operational detail
Intended useAnnex IV(1)(a): Intended purpose; out-of-scope usesYes — directly equivalent
Training dataAnnex IV(2)(a): Training data description and governanceYes — Annex IV requires data governance detail beyond provenance
Evaluation dataAnnex IV(2)(b): Validation and testing datasetsYes
Performance metricsAnnex IV(2)(c): Performance metrics; disaggregated resultsYes
Ethical considerationsAnnex IV(1)(f): Known risks and limitationsPartially — Annex IV requires FRIA reference
Limitations and caveatsAnnex IV(1)(e): Limitations on applicabilityYes
Not covered by model cardAnnex IV(3): Post-market monitoring plan; Annex IV(4): Logging and incident reporting procedures; Annex IV(7): Human oversight measuresNo — operational documentation required beyond model card

The practical conclusion is that a model card covers most of the model-level documentation required by EU AI Act Annex IV, but not the operational documentation. A compliant technical documentation package for a high-risk AI system requires a model card plus operational documentation covering post-market monitoring, logging, incident reporting, and human oversight procedures.
