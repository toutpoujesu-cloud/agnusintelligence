Algorithmic Impact Assessment: A Practical Guide

Published 11 Sep 2026
Reading time 14 min
Pillar AI Audit
Source EU AI Act Art. 27 · Canada AIDA · NYC Local Law 144

An Algorithmic Impact Assessment is a structured analysis of what an AI system will do to people — before it does it. The EU AI Act calls it a Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment. Canada's Algorithmic Impact Assessment tool calls it something similar. New York City's Local Law 144 mandates a bias audit before any employer uses an automated employment decision tool. The terminology differs. The obligation is the same: understand the harm before you deploy.

The legal landscape

Three distinct regulatory frameworks now mandate structured algorithmic impact assessment in different jurisdictions and contexts. Understanding which applies — and what each requires — is the first task for any organisation building or deploying AI systems that affect individuals.

EU AI Act — Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (Art. 27)

Article 27 of the EU AI Act requires certain deployers of high-risk AI systems to carry out a Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (FRIA) before the system is put into operation. The obligation applies to bodies governed by public law and to private entities providing public services — as well as to deployers in the employment, education, essential private services, and critical infrastructure sectors where the AI system affects members of the public.

The FRIA must describe: the deployer and the intended purpose of the AI system; the geographic and temporal scope of deployment; the categories of persons affected; the specific fundamental rights that may be affected; the likelihood and severity of harm; what measures the deployer intends to take to mitigate risks; a summary of the outcomes; and evidence that the FRIA was consulted when planning the deployment. The European Commission is required to provide a template for the FRIA — this template was under development at the time of writing.

SRC-0077 · EU AI Act Art. 27: FRIA requirement, scope, and content. Art. 27(9): template to be provided by the Commission.

Canada — Directive on Automated Decision-Making

Canada's Treasury Board Directive on Automated Decision-Making requires federal departments to complete an Algorithmic Impact Assessment before deploying any automated decision system. The AIA produces a score (1 to 4) that determines what controls are required — from basic procedural requirements at level 1 to independent audit and ministerial approval at level 4. Canada's AIA tool is publicly available and covers: rights implications, data governance, procedural fairness, and complexity.

The Canadian model is notable for its granularity: each question in the AIA is scored, and the aggregate score drives the requirement tier automatically. This makes the assessment more consistent across departments and harder to game than a purely qualitative assessment. Several other jurisdictions are examining this model.

SRC-0078 · Canada Treasury Board: Directive on Automated Decision-Making (2019, amended 2023). AIA tool available at canada.ca/en/government/system/digital-government/digital-government-innovations/responsible-use-ai.html

New York City — Local Law 144

New York City Local Law 144, effective January 2023, requires employers and employment agencies using an automated employment decision tool (AEDT) to conduct an annual bias audit before using the tool, and to publish a summary of the audit results. The bias audit must be conducted by an independent auditor and must test for disparate impact on the basis of sex, race/ethnicity, and intersectional categories. The audit must calculate selection rates and impact ratios for each protected category.

NYC Local Law 144 is the most prescriptive algorithmic impact assessment regime in force anywhere — it specifies exactly what must be tested, exactly who must conduct the test, and exactly what must be published. Its requirements have become de facto standards for employment AI auditing in the United States more broadly.

SRC-0079 · NYC Local Law 144 (2021): automated employment decision tools. NYC Rules Ch. 5: bias audit requirements and publication standards.

A six-phase AIA framework

The following framework synthesises requirements across the EU, Canadian, and NYC approaches. It is intended for organisations operating across multiple jurisdictions and needing a single assessment process that satisfies all three.

1
System description and intended use
Document what the system does, what decisions it supports or makes, who is affected, in what geographic and temporal scope. This is the foundation on which all subsequent analysis rests. Common failure: describing the system's technical architecture rather than its operational impact — the AIA is about people, not code.
2
Rights and impacts mapping
Identify which fundamental rights may be affected by the system's outputs. For EU purposes, the relevant rights framework is the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights. Common affected rights in AI systems: right to non-discrimination (Art. 21 Charter), right to data protection (Art. 8 Charter), right to an effective remedy (Art. 47 Charter), right to work (Art. 15 Charter), right to education (Art. 14 Charter). Not all will be relevant to every system — but all must be considered.
3
Disparate impact analysis
For each protected characteristic relevant to the system's use case, analyse whether the system produces systematically different outcomes for different groups. This requires access to data about the system's outputs stratified by protected characteristic — which in turn requires that those characteristics were recorded or can be inferred. For employment systems in NYC, this analysis must be conducted by an independent auditor and must calculate impact ratios as defined in Local Law 144. For EU systems, the analysis must cover all protected characteristics listed in Art. 21 of the Charter that are relevant to the use case.
4
Severity and likelihood assessment
For each identified risk, assess: how severe is the potential harm (reversible vs irreversible; individual vs systemic); how likely is it (near-certain vs plausible vs theoretical); who would be affected (vulnerable groups, children, elderly); and how many people could be affected at scale. The EU AI Act's risk classification system provides one model for this assessment — the distinction between minimal, limited, high, and unacceptable risk is a severity/likelihood matrix in disguise.
5
Mitigation measures
For each material risk identified, document: what mitigation has been implemented; what residual risk remains after mitigation; and what monitoring will be in place to detect if the mitigation fails. Mitigations must be specific — "we will monitor for bias" is not a mitigation; "we will calculate quarterly impact ratios by protected characteristic against the threshold defined in Schedule A, with automatic escalation to the AI governance lead if any ratio falls below 0.8" is a mitigation.
6
Approval, publication, and review
The completed AIA must be approved by a named individual with the authority to halt deployment if the risks are unacceptable. For EU public-sector deployers, this is typically the data protection officer and the accountable executive. Publication requirements: NYC Local Law 144 requires public summary publication; EU AI Act Art. 27(8) requires deployers to notify the market surveillance authority before deploying high-risk AI in certain public-sector contexts. Review cadence: annual for all deployed systems; immediate review triggered by any incident, material change to the system, or significant change to the operating context.

Common failures in AIA practice

Treating the AIA as a compliance checkbox. The most common failure is conducting an AIA after the deployment decision has been made, at a level of analysis insufficient to change anything. An AIA that cannot halt a deployment is not a governance control — it is paperwork. The AIA must be conducted before the deployment decision is finalised, and must be capable of producing a "do not deploy" outcome.

Insufficient data for disparate impact analysis. Many organisations discover, when attempting to conduct a disparate impact analysis, that they do not have the data needed to assess whether the system produces different outcomes for different groups. This is a data governance failure that predates the AIA — but it surfaces at AIA time. The response is not to proceed without the analysis; it is to obtain the data, commission independent testing, or defer deployment.

Scope creep between assessment and deployment. The AIA assesses the system as it will be deployed. If the deployment changes after the AIA — different data inputs, different population, different decision scope — the AIA must be updated. An AIA conducted for a pilot is not valid for a full deployment without reassessment of how scale changes the risk profile.

The FRIA is not a DPIA. The EU AI Act's Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment is not the same as a GDPR Data Protection Impact Assessment, though the two may overlap significantly for AI systems that process personal data. The DPIA assesses risks to data protection rights. The FRIA assesses risks to the broader set of fundamental rights in the EU Charter. Both may be required for the same system. Best practice is to conduct them jointly, with a single coordinated process, while keeping the outputs distinct to satisfy each regulatory requirement separately.
