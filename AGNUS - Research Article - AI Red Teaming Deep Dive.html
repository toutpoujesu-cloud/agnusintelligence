AI Red Teaming: How Organisations Test AI Systems Before They Fail in Production

Red teaming AI systems before deployment is the most effective method available for finding failure modes that standard testing misses — adversarial prompts, goal misalignment, jailbreaks, and emergent behaviours that only appear at scale. This article explains what AI red teaming is, how to structure it, and what the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF, and UK AISI guidance require.

AI red teaming consistently surfaces failure modes that automated testing does not catch. The EU AI Act's conformity assessment requirements for high-risk AI (Article 43) and NIST's AI RMF Govern, Map, and Measure functions all anticipate adversarial testing as part of a credible risk management programme.

What AI red teaming is — and is not

Red teaming originates in military strategy: a team assigned to think and act as the adversary, to find weaknesses that the blue team cannot see in their own plans. In AI, red teaming means systematically probing an AI system to find ways it fails, behaves unexpectedly, or can be made to produce harmful outputs — before those failures occur in production with real users.

Red teaming is not:

  • Penetration testing of the infrastructure — that is a cybersecurity exercise. Red teaming targets the model and its behaviour.
  • Standard QA or benchmark evaluation — those measure performance on known tasks. Red teaming specifically looks for failure in unknown or adversarial conditions.
  • A one-time activity — models drift, prompts evolve, and deployment contexts change. Red teaming is an ongoing programme, not a box to tick before launch.

The goal is adversarial discovery: find the inputs, sequences, and contexts that cause the model to fail in ways that matter to the organisation and its users.

Why standard testing is not enough

Standard testing validates that a system does what it is designed to do. Red teaming validates that it does not do things it should not do — a fundamentally different challenge. The space of possible harmful outputs is vast and poorly defined. You cannot write a test case for every failure mode you have not imagined yet.

Three properties of language models make this particularly important:

Property 1
Emergent capabilities
Large language models develop capabilities that were not explicitly trained for and that do not appear in smaller versions of the same model. These emergent capabilities — including some harmful ones — cannot be predicted from training data inspection alone. They must be discovered empirically.
Property 2
Context sensitivity
A model's behaviour in a controlled evaluation environment often differs from its behaviour when deployed with real users, real system prompts, and real downstream integrations. Failures that do not appear in testing frequently appear when the system is used by people actively trying to push its boundaries.
Property 3
Prompt injection vulnerability
AI agents that process external content — documents, web pages, email — are vulnerable to instructions embedded in that content directing the agent to behave contrary to its system prompt. This attack class is largely invisible to standard unit tests and requires specific adversarial probing to surface.
Regulatory requirement
EU AI Act conformity assessment
Article 43 requires high-risk AI systems to undergo conformity assessment before market placement. The assessment must include testing procedures demonstrating that the system meets the requirements of Chapter III, Section 2. Adversarial testing is the most credible way to demonstrate robustness compliance under Article 15.

Red teaming methods

Effective AI red teaming uses a layered set of methods, combining automated and human approaches:

Adversarial prompting

Human red teamers craft prompts specifically designed to elicit harmful, incorrect, or policy-violating outputs. This includes direct prompt injection (manipulative instructions in user input), indirect prompt injection (malicious instructions embedded in content the model processes), jailbreaking attempts, role-playing scenarios that lower the model's safety constraints, and edge cases at the boundaries of the system's intended use.

Automated red teaming

Tools like Microsoft's PyRIT and Garak can generate large volumes of adversarial inputs automatically, scale testing across many prompt variants, and systematically explore failure modes. Automated tools are most effective when combined with human judgement on which failures actually matter.

Multi-turn conversation testing

Many harmful behaviours only emerge after a sequence of turns — the model appears safe in single-turn evaluation but can be gradually led to harmful outputs through extended conversation. Red teamers must test multi-turn dynamics explicitly, not just individual prompts.

Agentic task testing

When AI systems can take actions — read files, call APIs, browse the web, write code — red teaming must cover the full action space. This includes testing whether the agent can be induced to take out-of-scope actions, exceed its authority, exfiltrate data, or bypass approval workflows. Agentic red teaming is substantially more complex than conversation testing.

How to run an AI red teaming programme

1

Scope definition

Define the system under test, its deployment context, and the harm categories in scope. A customer-facing chatbot has different risk priorities than an internal document analysis tool or an agentic coding assistant. Scope must be explicit: what failure modes are you looking for, in what contexts, with what severity thresholds?

2

Team composition

Effective red teams combine domain experts (who understand the deployment context), security researchers (who understand attack patterns), and diverse testers (who represent different user populations and perspectives). For regulated use cases, consider external red teamers who bring no institutional bias about how the system should behave.

3

Adversarial testing execution

Run both manual and automated testing in parallel. Document every finding with: the exact input, the model output, the harm category, the severity rating, and whether the finding is reproducible. Red teaming findings are evidence — they must be recorded with enough detail to reproduce, triage, and track to resolution.

4

Triage and remediation

Not every red team finding requires the same response. Classify findings by severity (critical / high / medium / low), by reproducibility, and by whether they represent a model-level issue (requires fine-tuning or system prompt changes) or a deployment-level issue (can be addressed by guardrails, output filters, or architectural changes). Track all findings to resolution with evidence.

5

Regression testing

After remediation, run the same adversarial inputs that produced failures to confirm they are resolved. When models are updated or system prompts change, re-run the full red team suite — model updates frequently introduce new failure modes while resolving old ones. Red teaming is a regression testing requirement, not a one-time gate.

6

Documentation and evidence

For EU AI Act high-risk systems, the technical documentation required under Article 11 (and Annex IV) must include the testing procedures used to demonstrate compliance with the robustness requirements of Article 15. Red teaming findings, remediation actions, and regression test results all belong in the technical documentation package.

Regulatory basis for AI red teaming

Red teaming is not explicitly required by name in most AI regulation, but the substantive requirements in multiple frameworks clearly anticipate it:

EU AI Act

Article 15 (Robustness, accuracy and cybersecurity) requires high-risk AI systems to achieve "an appropriate level of robustness, including with regard to attempts by unauthorised third parties to alter their use, outputs or performance." Testing robustness against attempts to alter behaviour is a description of adversarial testing. The conformity assessment procedures under Article 43 require demonstrating compliance with this requirement before market placement. (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Articles 15, 43.)

NIST AI RMF

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework's Measure function explicitly calls for "red-teaming, stress-testing, and adversarial testing" as part of AI risk assessment. The Govern function requires establishing processes for ongoing monitoring of AI systems, including for adversarial conditions. (NIST AI RMF 1.0, Measure 2.5.)

UK AISI

The UK AI Safety Institute has published evaluation frameworks specifically for frontier AI models that include adversarial testing for misuse potential and dangerous capabilities. While these focus on frontier models, the evaluation methodology is relevant for any organisation deploying capable AI systems at scale. (UK AISI evaluation framework.)

What red teaming typically finds

AGNUS Finding · AI Security

The most common red team findings in enterprise AI deployments are: (1) prompt injection via document content that overrides system prompt constraints; (2) role-playing or persona-switching that bypasses safety guardrails; (3) multi-turn conversation sequences that gradually extract restricted information; and (4) agentic task completion that takes unintended actions when user instructions conflict with system constraints. Of these, prompt injection in agentic systems is the most severe and the hardest to defend against architecturally.

Red teaming findings consistently show that models appear substantially safer in controlled evaluation than in production deployment with real users. The gap between benchmark performance and adversarial resilience is a fundamental property of current language model architectures — it cannot be engineered away through benchmarking alone. Adversarial testing is therefore not optional for organisations deploying AI systems in consequential contexts.
