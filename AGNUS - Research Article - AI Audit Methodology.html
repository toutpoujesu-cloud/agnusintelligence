How to Conduct an AI Audit: Methodology, Scope and Evidence Standards

An AI audit is a systematic, evidence-based assessment of an AI system against defined criteria. It differs from traditional IT auditing in one critical way: the model's decision-making process cannot be directly observed. This article maps a structured methodology for AI auditors and the evidence standards that findings must meet to be credible and defensible.

Jean-Daniel Alexis· 11 September 2026· AI Audit pillar· AR-000630

What AI auditing is — and what it is not

An AI audit is a formal, independent assessment of an AI system against a defined set of criteria. The criteria may come from regulation (EU AI Act compliance audit), from a standard (ISO 42001 certification audit), from internal policy (internal governance audit), or from a contractual obligation (vendor audit). The output is a documented set of findings — conformities, non-conformities, and recommendations — supported by evidence.

An AI audit is not: a performance evaluation (testing whether the model is accurate); a technical review (assessing the code quality); or a risk assessment (identifying potential harms). These activities may be inputs to an audit, but they are not audits in themselves. The distinguishing feature of an audit is that findings are made against stated criteria and supported by evidence that an independent reviewer could evaluate.

The evidence constraint is what makes AI auditing hard. In a financial audit, the auditor can trace a transaction from source document to financial statement. In an AI audit, the auditor cannot trace an output back to the model's "reasoning" — the internal weights that produced the output are not human-readable. Auditors work with what is observable: the model's inputs and outputs, the audit trail of actions taken, the documentation, the policies, and the processes that govern the system.

The five phases of an AI audit

1
Scope and criteria definition
Define the system being audited, the audit criteria (which regulation, standard, or policy), the audit period, and the audit questions. An audit with poorly defined scope will produce findings that are neither complete nor actionable. The criteria must be specific enough that a finding of non-conformity is unambiguous.
2
Documentation review
Review the system's documentation against the audit criteria: technical documentation, risk assessment, Mission Contracts, policies, training records, incident logs, test results. Documentation review identifies conformities and gaps — areas where required documentation does not exist or does not address what the criteria require.
3
Process and control testing
Test whether the controls documented on paper are actually operating in practice. Audit log sampling — reviewing a representative sample of the system's actions against its Mission Contract — tests whether the policy engine is functioning. Approval record review tests whether human approval gates are being honoured. Interview evidence tests whether responsible personnel understand their obligations.
4
Output and performance sampling
Sample the system's outputs against the performance claims in its documentation. For high-risk AI, this means testing accuracy, bias, and robustness claims against a representative dataset. The sample must be large enough and representative enough that findings are statistically meaningful. Output testing cannot prove the model is always correct — it can only provide evidence about its performance on the sampled distribution.
5
Findings, reporting and follow-up
Document findings as conformities (criteria are met, evidence available), non-conformities (criteria are not met, evidence of failure), or observations (no non-conformity but improvement recommended). Each non-conformity must cite the specific criterion not met and the specific evidence supporting the finding. The audit report must be sufficiently specific that a non-auditor can understand what is wrong and what must be done to remedy it.

Evidence standards for AI audits

Evidence typeWhat it demonstratesLimitations
Audit logsWhat actions the system actually took, when, and on whose instruction. The most direct evidence of system behaviour.Only as reliable as the logging infrastructure. If logs can be modified, they cannot be trusted as evidence. Append-only logs are required.
Technical documentationWhat the system was designed to do, how it was tested, what its known limitations are.Documents describe intent — they do not prove that the system performs as documented. Must be corroborated by output testing.
Output samplesHow the system actually behaves on a defined test set.Sampling cannot prove behaviour on all inputs. Results are only generalisable to the distribution tested.
Interview evidenceWhether responsible personnel understand their obligations and can describe the processes they operate.Weakest form of evidence — self-reported. Must be corroborated by documentary or testing evidence.
Incident and near-miss recordsWhether governance controls have been tested in practice and whether lessons have been incorporated.Absence of incidents does not demonstrate good governance — it may demonstrate poor detection.
Policy and training recordsWhether policies exist, are current, and have been communicated to relevant personnel.Policy existence does not prove policy compliance. Process testing is required to demonstrate operational effectiveness.

Findings and reporting standards

An AI audit finding must contain four elements to be actionable: the criterion not met (the specific regulatory requirement, standard clause, or policy provision); the evidence of non-conformity (what was observed that demonstrates the criterion is not met); the potential consequence (why this matters — what harm or non-compliance risk it creates); and the recommended corrective action (what must be done to achieve conformity).

The most common failure in AI audit reporting is findings that are too general. "The organisation lacks adequate AI governance documentation" is not a finding — it is a conclusion. A finding is: "EU AI Act Article 11 requires technical documentation for high-risk AI systems including a description of the system's intended purpose and the performance metrics used in testing. The audit found no technical documentation for the customer credit scoring system deployed in January 2026. Without this documentation, the system cannot demonstrate compliance with Article 11."

For external audits submitted to regulatory authorities, the evidence chain must be complete enough that the authority can independently verify the findings without access to the auditor. Each finding should be cross-referenced to the specific evidence document and location that supports it.
