Agentic AI and Autonomy Levels: What A1–A5 Mean for Governance

Autonomy is not binary. Between a fully supervised AI that confirms every action and a fully autonomous agent that acts without checking back, there is a spectrum with five meaningful levels. The level of autonomy you grant an agent determines what governance obligations attach — and what the EU AI Act requires.

Jean-Daniel Alexis · 11 September 2026 · AI Agents pillar · AR-000520

The five autonomy levels

There is no single authoritative taxonomy of AI autonomy levels. The A1–A5 framework used by AGNUS synthesises definitions from the NIST AI RMF, OECD AI Principles, and emerging practitioner literature. It is a practical governance tool, not a regulatory category.

A1
Fully supervised
Agent takes no action without explicit human confirmation. Every output is a proposal.
Human decides
A2
Human-confirmed
Agent acts autonomously within a pre-approved action set. Actions outside that set require human confirmation.
Human approves exceptions
A3
Human-monitored
Agent acts autonomously within its capability scope. A human monitors and can intervene. Thresholds trigger alerts.
Human monitors
A4
Human-advised
Agent acts autonomously and notifies humans after the fact. Humans can correct but cannot prevent most actions in real time.
Human informed
A5
Fully autonomous
Agent acts entirely independently. No human is in the loop for individual actions. Only system-level oversight applies.
System oversight only

Most commercial AI agent deployments today operate at A2 or A3. A1 is common during testing and onboarding. A4 and A5 are rare in regulated industries because the EU AI Act and analogous frameworks require meaningful human oversight for consequential decisions — which A4 and A5 by definition cannot provide at the action level.

What the EU AI Act says about autonomy

The EU AI Act does not use the A1–A5 framework or any equivalent. It does not define "autonomy levels" as a regulatory category. What it does do is set requirements that become progressively harder to satisfy at higher autonomy levels.

Article 14 (Human oversight) requires that high-risk AI systems be designed and developed so that natural persons can oversee and understand the system's outputs, intervene, and stop or suspend the system. This requirement applies at the system level, not the individual-action level — but it has practical implications for autonomy. A system operating at A4 (human informed after the fact) cannot satisfy Article 14's requirement for meaningful intervention on most actions, because the actions have already been taken before the human is aware of them.

The European Data Protection Board's March 2024 guidelines on automated decision-making under GDPR Article 22 add a further constraint: decisions that have a legal or similarly significant effect on individuals must involve human review. This effectively mandates A1 or A2 for those specific decisions, regardless of the operator's preference for higher autonomy.

The practical result is a two-tier structure for many organisations: A3 or A4 for routine, low-consequence actions; A1 or A2 for consequential decisions. The governance challenge is defining the boundary between these two categories clearly enough that the agent can route correctly at runtime.

Choosing an autonomy level in practice

The question is not "how autonomous should our agent be?" but "for which specific action types should we grant which level of autonomy?" The answer depends on four factors:

Consequence of error. An error in a draft email is consequential in a different way from an error in a financial transaction. The higher the consequence of an uncaught error, the lower the appropriate autonomy level for that action type.

Reversibility. Actions that can be undone warrant higher autonomy levels than actions that cannot. A file deletion and a payment transfer both have significant consequences, but a file in a versioned repository can be restored — a payment transfer is much harder to reverse.

Frequency. High-volume, low-consequence actions are strong candidates for A3. Rare, high-consequence actions are strong candidates for A1 or A2. The operational cost of requiring human approval on every action scales with volume — a design that requires A1 for 1,000 actions per hour is not a governance design; it is a bottleneck.

Regulatory exposure. Actions that trigger GDPR Article 22, EU AI Act Article 14 for high-risk AI, or sector-specific regulations (MiFID II, MDR) impose external constraints on autonomy level that override operational preferences.

Documenting the autonomy level assigned to each action type in the agent's Mission Contract is a governance best practice that serves two purposes: it makes the intended governance design explicit, and it creates a record of the decision that can be reviewed if something goes wrong.
