AI Security

AI Red Teaming: Testing AI Systems for Security and Misuse

Red teaming is the practice of systematically attempting to break an AI system — to find failure modes, safety boundaries, and security vulnerabilities before adversaries do. For AI security practitioners, red teaming is the highest-fidelity evaluation method available. This article covers what AI red teaming tests for, how it differs from traditional software security testing, the methodologies in use, and what regulators expect from red team programmes.

Contents

    What AI red teaming is — and what it is not

    Red teaming originated in military and intelligence practice: a designated team given the adversary's perspective and tasked with finding weaknesses in plans, systems, or defences. In AI security, red teaming means assigning a team — internal or external — to attempt to elicit harmful, unsafe, or unintended behaviour from an AI system through adversarial interaction.

    AI red teaming is not the same as conventional software penetration testing. Traditional software security testing looks for vulnerabilities in code — buffer overflows, injection flaws, authentication bypasses. These vulnerabilities have discrete, binary characteristics: the exploit either works or it does not. AI red teaming looks for failure modes in model behaviour — outputs that are harmful, misleading, biased, or unsafe — that are probabilistic, context-dependent, and often subtle. You can fix a buffer overflow with a patch. You cannot fix a tendency to produce harmful content with a patch — it requires retraining, fine-tuning, or system-level controls.

    Methodologies and approaches

    Manual red teaming

    Human red teamers interact with the system, attempting to elicit unsafe or unintended behaviour through creative, adversarial prompting. Manual red teaming is essential for finding novel failure modes that automated testing would not generate. It is resource-intensive and does not scale to large models across many topics without significant investment.

    Automated red teaming

    Automated red teaming uses AI systems to generate adversarial prompts at scale — systematically covering a defined risk taxonomy and generating thousands of test cases that manual testing could not produce. Automated approaches are effective for systematic coverage of known risk categories but are less effective at finding genuinely novel failure modes.

    Structured scenario testing

    Rather than open-ended adversarial interaction, structured scenario testing defines a set of specific scenarios — based on the system's use case — and tests the model's response to each. This is effective for use-case-specific risk assessment (a medical information system needs different scenarios than a customer service agent) and for regulatory conformity assessment, where evidence of structured testing against defined risk categories is required.

    What regulators expect

    The EU AI Act requires, for high-risk AI systems, that providers conduct testing "throughout the design and development process" and that testing be "in relation to the specific purpose for which the AI system is intended." For GPAI models with systemic risk, providers must perform adversarial testing — described in the EU AI Act's implementing regulation as including red teaming. The UK AI Security Institute has published frontier AI safety evaluation specifications that describe red teaming as a component of the evaluation framework for frontier models.

    In practice, regulators expect: a documented red team methodology; evidence that the red team had genuine adversarial intent (not just a compliance exercise); coverage of the risk categories relevant to the system's use case; and remediation of findings identified during the exercise.
