UK AISI finds that LLM elicited preferences are consistent — but do not reliably predict downstream task performance

The UK AI Security Institute evaluated five frontier LLMs across donation advice, refusal behaviour, and complex agentic tasks. Models showed consistent preferences across elicitation methods, but preference alignment did not predict performance on complex tasks — a critical finding for behavioural assurance frameworks.

Source: UK AI Security Institute research publication (SRC-0107, provisional ID — reconcile with live registry). This is an independent government research publication, not a regulatory requirement or guidance document. Findings are based on AISI's evaluation of five frontier models; results may not generalise to all model architectures. AGNUS does not provide legal advice.
Contents

    What the AISI studied

    The UK AI Security Institute (AISI) published research on 26 August 2026 investigating the relationship between elicited model preferences and downstream model behaviour. The study evaluated five frontier large language models across three categories of task: donation advice (testing whether preference-aligned advice emerged), refusal behaviour (testing preference-correlated refusals), and complex agentic tasks (testing whether elicited preferences predicted task performance).

    The research used two elicitation methods — asking models directly about their preferences and inferring preferences from model behaviour — to test whether the preferences identified were consistent across methods and whether those preferences predicted downstream outcomes.

    Three key findings

    1
    Preferences are consistent across elicitation methods

    All five models showed consistent preferences when the same preferences were elicited using two different methods. This means that elicited model preferences are a stable property of the model — they are not an artefact of a particular elicitation approach. This is relevant to assurance: the same preference profile will emerge regardless of how you elicit it.

    2
    Preferences predict advice and refusals — but inconsistently

    Preference-aligned advice (where the model's output in donation advice reflected its elicited preferences) was observed. Preference-correlated refusals (where models refused tasks that conflicted with their elicited preferences) were also observed. However, the relationship was not uniform across models or task types.

    3
    Preferences do not predict performance on complex agentic tasks

    On complex agentic tasks — where models were required to plan, execute multi-step actions, and navigate ambiguous situations — no preference-driven difference in performance was found. The model's elicited preferences did not predict whether it would succeed or fail at complex tasks. This is the most significant finding for those building assurance frameworks around model preferences.

    What this means for behavioural assurance

    The AISI research has direct implications for organisations building behavioural assurance frameworks for AI systems. The conventional assumption is that if you understand a model's preferences — what it values, what it prioritises, what it refuses — you can predict how it will behave in deployment. The AISI findings complicate this assumption in two important ways.

    First, preference consistency is real: a model's preferences are stable and reproducible. If you elicit them, you get the same answer regardless of method. This is useful for assurance — preferences can be characterised reliably.

    Second, preference consistency does not translate to behavioural predictability on complex tasks. The fact that a model has a stable preference profile does not mean that preference will be expressed consistently across all types of downstream task — particularly complex agentic tasks involving multi-step reasoning and real-world action.

    Assurance implication: Governance frameworks that rely primarily on elicited model preferences as a proxy for deployment safety need to account for the AISI finding that preferences are a poor predictor of complex agentic task behaviour. Behavioural assurance for agentic AI systems requires task-specific testing, not preference elicitation alone.

    What organisations should watch

    • Follow-on AISI research on the mechanisms behind the preference/performance gap — particularly for agentic and multi-step tasks
    • Whether the EU AI Act's conformity assessment requirements for high-risk AI systems incorporate task-specific behavioural testing as a result of findings like these
    • Updates to the UK AI Safety Institute's evaluation framework for frontier models, which may integrate these findings into the AISI's standard evaluation protocols
