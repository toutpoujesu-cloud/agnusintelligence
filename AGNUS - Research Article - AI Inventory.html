AI Inventory: How to Catalogue Every AI System in Your Organisation

Published 11 Sep 2026
Reading time 15 min
Pillar AI Governance
Source EU AI Act Art. 26 · ISO 42001 · NIST AI RMF

Most organisations that believe they are deploying AI responsibly cannot name every AI system they operate. An AI inventory — a structured register of every system, its risk level, its responsible owner, and its governance status — is the first document any governance programme must produce. Without it, nothing else in AI governance is reliable.

Why an AI inventory is the foundation of governance

You cannot govern what you cannot see. The EU AI Act requires deployers of high-risk AI systems to maintain documentation under Article 26 and to implement human oversight measures. The UK AI Management Essentials framework requires organisations to identify and classify AI systems. ISO 42001, the international AI management system standard, requires a structured inventory as part of its control framework. Every major AI governance standard begins with the same question: what AI systems do you operate?

The answer is almost always more than expected. A financial services firm that believes it operates three AI systems typically discovers, on first inventory, that it operates twelve or more — including systems embedded in SaaS tools it procures from third parties, AI features activated by default in productivity software, and legacy systems that were not initially recognised as AI. The EU AI Act's definition of an AI system is deliberately broad: a machine-based system that infers outputs — predictions, recommendations, or decisions — from input it receives. Many tools that organisations do not think of as AI meet this definition.

The EU AI Act problem. If your organisation operates high-risk AI (listed in Annex III) and has not completed an AI inventory, you are not in a position to meet your obligations under Article 26. The AI Act does not require you to have solved every governance question — but it does require you to know which systems you operate and what risk class they fall into.

What goes in an AI inventory entry

A minimal AI inventory entry contains eight fields. An extended entry adds seven more. Below is the full set recommended for organisations operating under the EU AI Act, with rationale for each field.

System nameThe official or common name of the system, including version where relevant.Identifies the system unambiguously across the organisation.
System IDA unique internal reference (e.g. AI-001). Used in audit trails, incident records, and policy documents.Enables traceability. Critical when a system changes name, vendor, or version.

Extended fields (recommended for high-risk AI)

For systems classified as high-risk under Annex III, the following additional fields are required to meet EU AI Act obligations in full.

FieldWhat to record
Intended purposeThe provider's defined intended purpose for the system, as stated in the instructions for use. This is the use case the system was validated for — deviations may constitute misuse under Art. 26(1).
Training data sourceWhere the training data came from and whether any data governance obligations apply (GDPR basis, retention, etc.).

How to conduct your first AI inventory

  1. Scope the exercise clearly before beginning. Decide upfront whether you are inventorying only systems built in-house, or also systems procured from third parties. The EU AI Act applies to both — you are a deployer for every system you put into operation, regardless of who built it. Include AI features embedded in productivity software (e.g. Microsoft 365 Copilot, Google Workspace AI), provided they process your organisation's data or affect your organisation's operations.
  2. Use four discovery channels simultaneously. The four most effective channels for AI discovery are: (1) IT procurement records — any purchase order that included AI, machine learning, or intelligent features; (2) SaaS contracts — most enterprise SaaS now embeds AI by default; (3) team leads — direct questions to departmental heads about tools they have introduced, including freemium tools not procured through IT; (4) developer repositories — internal tools built by your engineering team. No single channel is sufficient.
  3. Apply the EU AI Act definition as your threshold. A system qualifies for the inventory if it makes inferences — predictions, recommendations, or decisions — from input data. A rule-based system with deterministic if/then logic does not qualify. A system that uses ML, neural networks, statistical modelling, or Bayesian inference does. When in doubt, include it — the cost of including something that turns out to be out of scope is negligible; the cost of missing a high-risk system is significant.
  4. Classify each system at first pass using the simplified EU AI Act risk ladder. Unacceptable risk (banned practices under Art. 5) — must not be in operation. High-risk (Annex III list) — requires full compliance programme. Limited risk — transparency obligations apply. Minimal risk — good practice controls are sufficient, no specific obligations. Flag anything where classification is uncertain: this requires legal review before going live.
  5. Assign an owner to every entry before the inventory is considered complete. An inventory entry without a named human owner is not a governance control — it is an unmanaged risk. The owner must be someone with the authority and information to answer questions about the system, initiate its decommissioning if required, and respond to a regulatory enquiry. A team name or department is not sufficient.
  6. Set review dates and build the review into your governance calendar. An inventory reviewed once and never again becomes inaccurate within months. High-risk AI systems should be reviewed every six months at minimum. All other systems should be reviewed annually. The review should ask: is this system still operating? Has its use changed? Has the provider updated it in ways that affect its risk classification or conformity status? Has an incident occurred that should be recorded?
  7. Link the inventory to your incident management and procurement processes. The inventory is only useful if it is a live document. This requires two integrations: first, any new AI procurement must trigger an inventory entry before deployment; second, any AI-related incident must be cross-referenced to the inventory entry for the relevant system. Without these integrations, the inventory becomes historical and loses its value as a governance control.

What the EU AI Act requires from your inventory

The EU AI Act does not mandate a specific format for an AI inventory, but it does mandate — across Articles 13, 26, 27, and 72 — a set of record-keeping obligations from which a coherent inventory is the only practical response. Article 26 requires deployers of high-risk AI to implement appropriate human oversight measures and maintain logs where technically feasible. Article 27 requires deployers in certain sectors to conduct a Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment before deployment. Article 72 requires post-market monitoring plans for providers — for deployers, this translates to the need to know what systems you are running and whether their performance matches expectations.

Practically, any regulator enquiry, any audit under ISO 42001, and any procurement due diligence process will begin with the same question: show us your AI inventory. An organisation that cannot produce one within 48 hours of being asked is demonstrably not in control of its AI systems — regardless of how many governance documents it has written.

Key regulatory positions on AI inventory requirements: The EU AI Act Art. 26(6) requires deployers to retain the Declaration of Conformity for high-risk systems for 10 years. ISO 42001 Clause 8.4 requires a documented inventory of AI systems as part of the AI management system. The UK Government's AI Management Essentials (2024) lists 'know what AI you have' as its first control. The NIST AI RMF Govern 1.1 function requires mapping of AI use cases and their contexts. These are not advisory — for regulated organisations, they are compliance requirements.

Common inventory failures and how to avoid them

Treating the inventory as a one-time exercise. An inventory completed once and not maintained is worse than no inventory — it creates false confidence. The inventory must be a living document with defined review triggers (new procurement, system update, incident, annual review).

Excluding third-party AI. The EU AI Act applies to every system you put into operation, not only systems you built. If you use a third-party AI tool in a business process, you are the deployer and the obligations of Art. 26 apply to you. The provider's obligations are separate and do not discharge yours.

Classifying all AI as minimal risk to avoid obligations. This is the most common failure in first inventories and the most legally dangerous. If a regulator later determines that a system you classified as minimal risk was in fact high-risk under Annex III, the absence of the required controls will be an aggravating factor in any enforcement action. When in doubt, classify conservatively and seek legal review.

Recording the system name but not the purpose. System names are rarely self-explanatory. "SmartHire" or "RiskScore 2.0" tell a regulator nothing about what the system does, who it affects, or what risk class it falls into. The primary use field must describe what the system does in plain language, in a way that is comprehensible to a non-specialist.

Failing to include AI embedded in SaaS. The most significant inventory gap in most organisations is AI features embedded in tools they already use. Microsoft 365 Copilot, Salesforce Einstein, Workday AI, ServiceNow's generative features — these are AI systems under the EU AI Act if they process your employees' or customers' data. Whether they constitute high-risk AI depends on their specific use case in your organisation.

The inventory as a management tool

The AI inventory is not primarily a compliance document — it is a management tool. An organisation that knows exactly which AI systems it operates, who is responsible for each, what they are permitted to do, and when they were last reviewed is in a fundamentally different governance position from one that does not. The inventory is the foundation on which everything else — risk assessments, fundamental rights impact assessments, human oversight mechanisms, procurement due diligence, incident management — rests.

The most effective AI inventories are embedded in existing governance processes rather than maintained as standalone documents. The ideal state is one in which no AI system can be procured without an inventory entry, no incident can be recorded without a link to an inventory entry, and no board AI report is presented without drawing on the inventory for its data. Reaching that state takes time — but the first inventory, however imperfect, is the necessary first step.
