UK DSIT study identifies significant evidence gaps in cybersecurity governance of open-source AI

A commissioned study reviewed 14,561 academic records and 172 grey-literature records to map the evidence base on cybersecurity risks in open-source AI. The key finding: significant evidence gaps exist — especially in upstream governance — that current practice cannot close.

Source: UK Department for Science, Innovation and Technology — commissioned research publication (SRC-0108, provisional). This is independent commissioned research, not UK government policy or guidance. It does not impose any regulatory requirements. AGNUS does not provide legal advice.
Contents

    What the study examined

    The UK Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (DSIT) commissioned an independent research study — published on 7 September 2026 — examining the cybersecurity literature on open-source software (OSS) and AI. The study is not UK government policy; it is commissioned research that informs the evidence base for future policy development.

    Open-source AI presents a distinct governance challenge from proprietary AI: the model weights, architecture, and often the training code are publicly available, meaning that traditional supply-chain security controls based on vendor relationships cannot apply in the same way. The study was designed to map what the evidence base actually says about cybersecurity risks in this area — and, critically, where the evidence base has gaps.

    Key findings

    Evidence gaps — not just knowledge gaps

    The study's primary finding is that significant evidence gaps exist in the cybersecurity literature on open-source AI. An evidence gap is more serious than a knowledge gap: it means not just that we do not know the answer to a question, but that the research needed to generate the evidence has not been done. This matters for governance because regulators and standards bodies typically require evidence before imposing requirements.

    Upstream governance is the most poorly evidenced area

    The study identifies upstream governance — the security of the supply chain, training data, model weights, and model development processes that precede deployment — as the most significant evidence gap. Most existing cybersecurity research on open-source AI focuses on deployed systems, not on the processes that produce them. This creates a structural blind spot in current assurance frameworks.

    GitHub and Hugging Face data analysed

    The study included analysis of GitHub and Hugging Face data — the two primary distribution platforms for open-source AI models and code. This analysis examined how open-source AI artefacts are shared, modified, and deployed in practice, and identified specific governance challenges in these distribution ecosystems.

    Implication for assurance: Organisations deploying open-source AI models cannot rely solely on published cybersecurity literature to design their assurance frameworks. The evidence gaps identified in the DSIT study mean that organisations need to conduct their own upstream governance assessments — the academic evidence base is not sufficient to answer the key questions about open-source AI supply-chain security.

    What organisations should do

    • Upstream supply-chain assessment: assess the provenance, integrity, and modification history of open-source AI models you deploy — the DSIT study confirms that general literature-based assurance is insufficient for upstream governance
    • Hugging Face and GitHub governance: if you source models from these platforms, implement controls on model verification, scanning, and provenance validation before deployment
    • Monitor for DSIT policy development: this study is commissioned research that feeds into future UK AI cybersecurity policy — track whether it leads to guidance, standards requirements, or regulation
    • EU AI Act interaction: the EU AI Act's supply-chain requirements for high-risk AI providers and deployers will likely intersect with open-source AI governance — monitor for Commission guidance on how open-source model use affects conformity assessment obligations
